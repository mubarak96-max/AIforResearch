# Sleep duration and examination performance in first-year university students: a cross-sectional study

## Abstract

**Background:** Short sleep is common among university students and may affect academic performance. **Methods:** We surveyed first-year students at two universities and linked self-reported sleep duration to end-of-semester examination marks. **Results:** Of 412 students, those sleeping less than six hours had lower marks than those sleeping seven to eight hours. **Conclusions:** Short sleep is associated with lower examination performance in first-year students.

## Introduction

Sleep supports memory consolidation, and laboratory studies show that sleep after learning improves later recall. Surveys in several countries report that between a third and a half of university students sleep less than the recommended seven hours. Whether this translates into poorer examination results in the first year, when students adjust to independent study, is less clear. This study aimed to estimate the association between habitual sleep duration and end-of-semester examination marks in first-year students.

## Methods

### Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of first-year undergraduate students at two public universities between March and May 2025.

### Participants

All students enrolled in the first year of any degree programme were eligible. Students were invited by email and through lecture announcements. Students who were repeating the year were excluded.

### Variables and measurement

Habitual sleep duration on weekdays was measured with a single question from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and grouped as less than 6 hours, 6 to 7 hours, and 7 to 8 hours or more. The outcome was the mean mark across all end-of-semester examinations, obtained from university records with the students' consent. We also recorded age, sex, degree programme, paid work hours and caffeine use.

### Statistical analysis

Marks were compared across sleep groups with one-way ANOVA. Linear regression estimated the difference in mean mark between sleep groups, adjusting for age, sex, programme and paid work hours. Analyses used R version 4.4.

## Results

A total of 412 students completed the survey. Their mean age was 19.4 years and 58% were women. Overall, 31% reported sleeping less than 6 hours on weekdays.

Students sleeping less than 6 hours had a mean mark of 58.2, compared with 63.9 among those sleeping 7 to 8 hours or more. After adjustment, short sleep was associated with a mark 4.8 points lower (95% CI 2.1 to 7.5) than the reference group. Paid work of more than 15 hours a week was also associated with lower marks.

## Discussion

Short sleep was common and was associated with lower examination marks, independent of paid work and programme. This is consistent with laboratory evidence on sleep and memory. Universities should consider sleep education in first-year orientation.

The study relied on self-reported sleep. Because the design is cross-sectional, we cannot say whether short sleep causes lower marks.

## Conclusion

One in three first-year students sleeps less than six hours, and these students score lower in examinations.
