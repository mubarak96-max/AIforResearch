Title: Green Space, Everyday Mobility and Mental Wellbeing of University Students in Nairobi: A Mixed-Methods Study

Degree: Doctor of Philosophy in Environmental Studies

Abstract

Evidence linking urban green space to mental health comes mostly from high-income countries, and little is known about how students in rapidly growing African cities encounter and use green space. This thesis used a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design across three public university campuses in Nairobi. In the quantitative phase, 612 undergraduate students completed the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale and the Perceived Stress Scale, reported weekly green-space visits, and gave their residential location. Green space exposure was measured as mean NDVI within 300-metre and 500-metre buffers using Sentinel-2 imagery. Multilevel regression models with students nested in campuses showed that each 0.1 increase in NDVI within 500 metres was associated with a 1.9-point higher wellbeing score (95% CI 0.8 to 3.0), adjusting for age, gender, year of study, household income and commuting time. Visiting green space at least twice a week was associated with lower perceived stress, and the association was stronger for women. In the qualitative phase, 28 students took part in walking interviews and six focus groups. Thematic analysis identified three themes: green space as respite from crowded accommodation, perceived insecurity limiting use of parks, and campus grounds as the most accessible green space. The thesis contributes evidence from an African urban setting, shows that perceived safety shapes whether green space benefits are realised, and recommends that universities treat campus green space as part of student mental health provision.

Chapters: 1 Introduction; 2 Literature review and theoretical framework (attention restoration theory, stress reduction theory, the pathways framework); 3 Study area and methods; 4 Quantitative results; 5 Qualitative findings; 6 Integrated discussion; 7 Conclusions and policy implications.
