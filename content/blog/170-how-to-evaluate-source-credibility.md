# How to evaluate source credibility: CRAAP, lateral reading, SIFT and the checks scholars use

*Last updated: 14 November 2026*

To evaluate a source's credibility, check who made it and what others say about them (lateral reading), whether it went through peer review or editorial checks, whether its evidence can be traced, and whether it has been retracted, corrected or superseded. Use the CRAAP test for scholarly sources and SIFT for anything you find on the open web. Judge credibility for each claim you use the source to support, not once for the whole source.

This guide is part of our series on essays, theses and sources. It covers the evaluation methods, what peer review does and does not guarantee, and the three problems that catch researchers most often: predatory journals, retracted papers and preprints. For where to find sources in the first place, see [how to find sources for a research paper](/blog/how-to-find-sources-for-a-research-paper).

## What makes a source credible?

**A source is credible when its authors have relevant expertise, a checking process stands behind it, its evidence can be traced, it is current for your question, and its purpose is clear.** No single sign is enough. A peer-reviewed paper can be retracted; a government report can be out of date; a blog post by a leading researcher can be the best explanation available.

| Signal | What to check | Strong | Weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Expertise | Author's training, position and publication record in this topic | A virologist writing on viral transmission | A celebrity, or an expert writing outside their field |
| Process | Peer review, editorial review or official approval | A journal with a stated peer review policy | No editor, no review, no named author |
| Evidence | Data, methods and references you can follow | Methods described, data or references linked | Claims with no source, or sources that do not say what is claimed |
| Currency | Date, and whether newer work has changed the picture | Recent, or a classic still accepted | Superseded, retracted or corrected |
| Purpose | Who funds it and why it exists | Disclosed funding, aim to inform | Hidden sponsor, aim to sell or persuade |

Being primary or secondary is a separate question from being credible. See [primary vs secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources) for that distinction, and [levels of evidence](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) for how study design affects how much a finding can bear.

## What is the CRAAP test?

**The CRAAP test is a five-part checklist (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) created by Sarah Blakeslee at the Meriam Library, California State University, Chico** [1]. It is the most widely taught source checklist in college libraries.

| Letter | Question | Worked example (illustrative): a 2019 report on e-cigarette use by teenagers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Currency** | When was it published or updated? Is that recent enough for this topic? | 2019 is old for a fast-moving topic; look for newer national survey data |
| **Relevance** | Does it address your question, for your population and setting? | It covers one country; your paper compares three |
| **Authority** | Who wrote and published it? What are their credentials? | A national public health agency: strong authority on prevalence |
| **Accuracy** | Is it based on evidence? Can you trace the data? Do other sources agree? | Based on a named national survey with published methods |
| **Purpose** | Why does it exist? To inform, sell, persuade or entertain? Is funding disclosed? | Public information; no commercial sponsor |

**Verdict:** credible for that country's prevalence in 2019; find newer data and comparable surveys for the other two countries.

### What are the limits of the CRAAP test?

**CRAAP checks are done on the page, and a well-designed site can pass them while hiding who is behind it.** An "About" page can claim expertise, list credentials and show a professional design. Research by Wineburg and McGrew found that checking a site by reading its own pages closely, the way many checklists encourage, often led readers to trust sources they should have questioned [2]. Use CRAAP for scholarly sources and structured documents. For websites, add lateral reading.

## What is lateral reading?

**Lateral reading means leaving a source to see what other sources say about it, instead of evaluating it from the inside.** The term comes from a study by Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew of the Stanford History Education Group, who watched professional fact-checkers, historians and Stanford undergraduates evaluate websites [2].

The fact-checkers did best. Within seconds of landing on an unfamiliar site, they opened new browser tabs and searched for the organization and the author. Historians and students tended to read *vertically*, staying on the site and judging it by its look, its "About" page and its references. In one task, many participants could not tell a long-established professional medical body from a small advocacy group with a similar name, because both sites looked authoritative [2]. <!-- VERIFY: the study compared 10 historians, 10 fact-checkers and 25 Stanford students, and the task compared the American Academy of Pediatrics with the American College of Pediatricians; confirm before adding these details. -->

Later classroom assessments by the same group found that most students struggled to judge online sources, for example by failing to notice who had sponsored content [3]. <!-- VERIFY: the Stanford History Education Group's civic online reasoning work moved to the Digital Inquiry Group in 2024; confirm the current name before mentioning it. -->

### How to read laterally (worked example, illustrative)

You find a site, "Institute for Healthy Soil Policy", with a report saying a fertilizer ban will halve crop yields.

1. **Leave the site.** Open a new tab and search the institute's name plus "funding", then read what news reports and registry records say about it.
2. **Check the author.** Search the report's author: do they publish in peer-reviewed soil science journals?
3. **Decide.** If you find that the institute is funded by a fertilizer trade group, the report may still contain data, but you should not rely on it alone. Find peer-reviewed field studies on the same question.

## What is the SIFT method?

**SIFT is a four-move routine for checking online claims quickly: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original context.** It was developed by Mike Caulfield, building on his open textbook *Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers* and on lateral reading [4][5].

| Move | What you do | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S**top | Before you read closely or use it, ask: do I know this source? What do I want from it? | Seconds |
| **I**nvestigate the source | Read laterally: what do other sources say about the publisher or author? | About a minute |
| **F**ind better coverage | If the source is weak, look for a more trusted source reporting the same claim | A minute or two |
| **T**race claims, quotes and media | Follow a statistic, quotation or image back to where it first appeared and check it was reported accurately | A few minutes |

The last move matters most in academic work. A statistic in a news article or a review usually comes from a study. Trace it, open the study, and check that the number, population and conditions match. Our guide on [how to check a claim against the research](/blog/how-to-check-a-claim-against-the-research) walks through that step in detail.

### CRAAP or SIFT: which should you use?

| Situation | Use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A journal article, scholarly book or official report | CRAAP, plus a retraction check | The publication process is visible, so on-page checks are informative |
| An unfamiliar website, blog or organization | SIFT (lateral reading) | Who is behind it is not visible on the page |
| A statistic, quote or image in the news or on social media | SIFT, especially Trace | You need the original context |
| A preprint | CRAAP, plus a check for a published version | No peer review yet |

## Does peer review make a source credible?

**Peer review makes a source more likely to be sound, but it does not guarantee that the findings are correct.** In peer review, editors send a manuscript to independent experts who judge its methods, analysis and conclusions before publication.

Peer review catches many problems, but reviewers rarely see raw data, cannot rerun experiments, and can miss errors, weak statistics and fraud. Richard Smith, a former editor of *The BMJ*, summarized the evidence as showing that peer review is slow, expensive, inconsistent and poor at detecting errors, while still being the system science relies on [6].

How to check:

- **Find the journal's peer review policy** on its website ("Peer review process", "For authors" or "Editorial policies").
- **Check the article type.** Editorials, letters, commentaries and news items in a journal are often not peer reviewed.
- **Read the paper anyway.** Peer review is a minimum, not a verdict. Our guide on [how to read a research paper](/blog/how-to-read-a-research-paper) covers critical reading.

## How do you spot a predatory journal?

**A predatory journal takes publication fees but skips or fakes peer review and misrepresents its practices.** An international panel defined predatory journals and publishers as entities that "prioritize self-interest at the expense of scholarship and are characterized by false or misleading information, deviation from best editorial and publication practices, a lack of transparency, and/or the use of aggressive and indiscriminate solicitation practices" [7]. <!-- VERIFY: exact wording of the Grudniewicz et al. (2019) consensus definition quoted here. -->

| Warning sign | Example (illustrative) |
| --- | --- |
| Aggressive email invitations to submit, often outside your field | "Dear Esteemed Author, submit to our journal within 7 days" |
| Very fast acceptance, or a promise of publication in days | "Peer review in 72 hours guaranteed" |
| A broad scope that does not fit together | "International Journal of Science, Medicine, Business and Engineering" |
| No clear editorial board, or board members who do not list it | Names you cannot connect to the journal on their university pages |
| Claimed indexing or impact factors that do not check out | "Indexed in Google Scholar", or a made-up metric |

How to check a journal:

1. **Look it up in the Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ)** if it is open access [8]. DOAJ vets journals before listing them.
2. **Check indexing claims directly.** For health journals, search the NLM Catalog to see whether the journal is indexed in MEDLINE.
3. **Check the publisher's memberships**, such as the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), on the member organization's own site.
4. **Use the Think. Check. Submit. checklist** [9], designed for authors choosing a journal but useful for readers too.

Open access is not a warning sign in itself; many leading journals are open access.

## How do you check if a paper has been retracted?

**Search the paper's title in the Retraction Watch Database, and check the article page on the publisher's site for a retraction notice, expression of concern or correction.** A journal retracts a paper when its findings are unreliable, because of error or misconduct, or when it breaches publication ethics. Guidelines from the Committee on Publication Ethics describe when journals should retract, and that retracted articles should stay online, clearly marked [10].

The Retraction Watch Database is the largest public list of retractions. Crossref acquired it in 2023 and made it openly available [11]. <!-- VERIFY: date and terms of the Crossref acquisition of the Retraction Watch Database (announced September 2023). -->

| Notice | What it means | Can you cite it as evidence? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retraction | The journal no longer stands by the paper | No. Cite it only to discuss the retraction, and say it was retracted |
| Expression of concern | The journal has doubts and is investigating | With great caution, and say so |
| Correction (erratum, corrigendum) | An error was fixed; the paper stands | Yes, citing the corrected version |

**A real example.** Wakefield and colleagues' 1998 paper in *The Lancet*, which suggested a link between the MMR vaccine and autism, was fully retracted by the journal in 2010 [12]. It is still discussed and sometimes cited. If you mention it, cite it as a retracted paper and cite the retraction notice.

Retracted papers keep collecting citations, so check every reference in your final list, not just the ones you doubt.

## Are preprints credible sources?

**Preprints are research papers posted publicly before peer review, so they are early findings, not settled ones.** Servers such as arXiv (physics, mathematics, computer science), bioRxiv (biology) and medRxiv (health sciences) screen submissions for basic suitability but do not peer review them. medRxiv states on its site that its preprints have not been peer reviewed and should not guide clinical practice [13].

Use a preprint when:

- It is the newest or only evidence on a fast-moving question.
- You label it as a preprint in the text and the reference.
- You have checked for a peer-reviewed version. Preprint pages often link to the published article; you can also search the title.

If a published version exists, cite it instead, and check whether the conclusions changed during review.

## A five-minute credibility check for any source

1. **Stop.** What is the source, and what claim do I want it to support?
2. **Investigate.** Read laterally about the author and publisher (SIFT), or check authority and purpose (CRAAP).
3. **Check the process.** Peer reviewed? Journal legitimate? Preprint?
4. **Check its status.** Retracted, corrected or under an expression of concern?
5. **Trace and match.** Does the original study say what you are about to claim, for the same population and conditions?

## FAQ

### What makes a source credible?

A credible source is written by people with relevant expertise, published through a process that checks it, based on evidence you can trace, current enough for your question, and honest about its purpose. In academic work that usually means peer-reviewed articles, scholarly books and official reports. Credibility is not a label on the source: you judge it for each claim you use it to support.

### What is the CRAAP test?

The CRAAP test is a checklist for evaluating sources, created by librarian Sarah Blakeslee at California State University, Chico. It asks about Currency (when it was published), Relevance (whether it fits your need), Authority (who wrote it), Accuracy (whether the evidence holds up) and Purpose (why it exists). It works well for scholarly sources, but checks on the page alone can be fooled by a professional-looking website.

### What is lateral reading?

Lateral reading means leaving a website to find out what other sources say about it, instead of judging it from its own pages. Researchers at the Stanford History Education Group found that professional fact-checkers did this within seconds, opening new tabs to search the organization and author, and judged sources more accurately than historians and Stanford students who read the site from top to bottom.

### What is the SIFT method?

SIFT is a four-step routine for checking online information, developed by Mike Caulfield. Stop before you read or share; Investigate the source, briefly, by reading laterally; Find better coverage, looking for a more trusted source on the same claim; and Trace claims, quotes and media back to their original context. Each step takes a minute or less.

### Can I cite a retracted paper?

Only to discuss the retraction itself, and then you should say clearly that the paper was retracted and cite the retraction notice. Do not cite a retracted paper as evidence for a finding. Check every source before you submit, because retracted papers keep being cited for years. The Retraction Watch Database and the publisher's article page show whether a paper has been retracted.

### Are preprints credible sources?

Preprints can be useful, but they have not been peer reviewed, so treat them as provisional. Cite one only when it is the best evidence available, label it as a preprint in your reference, and check before you submit whether a peer-reviewed version has been published. If it has, cite the published version, and note whether the findings changed.
