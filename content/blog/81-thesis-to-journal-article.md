# How to turn a thesis into a journal article

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

To turn a thesis into a journal article, pick one question your thesis answers, choose a target journal, and rewrite that chapter as a 4,000 to 8,000 word paper in the journal's format. Cut the literature review to the gap, keep only the analyses that answer the question, and tell the editor the work comes from your thesis. Most publishers accept this.

This guide is for recent master's and PhD graduates, and students in a final year, who want a paper out of their thesis. It belongs to our series on reviewing and publishing; the pillar is [how to respond to reviewer comments](/blog/how-to-respond-to-reviewer-comments), which you will need after you submit.

## Can you publish your thesis as a journal article?

Yes, in most fields and with most publishers, as long as you rewrite it as an article and disclose its origin. Elsevier states that it "does not view an abstract, academic thesis or preprint to be prior publication" [2]. Nature's editors have said that "a doctoral thesis does not count as 'previously published'" [3]. The APA notes that a dissertation or thesis is often submitted, with changes, as a journal manuscript [1].

Two exceptions to check first:

- **Journal policies differ.** Some journals and imprints set their own rules on prior publication. Read the target journal's guide for authors [2].
- **Copyright.** If your thesis was published as a book, or you signed rights to a third party, a journal may need that party's permission [2].

## What changes between a thesis chapter and an article?

**A journal article is a short, self-contained report of one research question for specialist readers, while a thesis is a long demonstration to examiners that you can do research.** That difference drives every edit.

| | Thesis | Journal article |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader | Examiners checking competence | Specialists who want the finding fast |
| Length | 40,000 to 100,000 words in total | Often 4,000 to 8,000 words; check the journal |
| Questions | Several, across chapters | One, sometimes two closely linked |
| Literature review | A full chapter showing you know the field | Two to four paragraphs leading to the gap |
| Method | Every step, every decision justified | Enough to replicate; detail in supplements |
| Results | All analyses, including null and side results | The analyses that answer the question |
| Tone | Shows you know the limits of everything | States the claim, then its limits |

## How to turn a thesis into a journal article: seven steps

1. **Pick the one claim.** Write the article's main finding in one sentence. If you can't, the chapter holds more than one paper or none yet.
2. **Choose the journal before you write.** Read three recent articles in it that are close to yours. Note their length, structure, how long the introduction is and how they report methods. Check scope, open-access fees and typical review time on the journal's site.
3. **Set a word budget.** Use the journal's limit and the proportions in the example below.
4. **Rebuild the introduction.** Start from the problem, summarize what is known in a few paragraphs, state the gap and your question. Cut every study that doesn't lead to the gap. The APA notes that condensing the literature review is usually the main challenge [1]. <!-- VERIFY: APA page wording; page did not render in automated fetch -->
5. **Trim the method and results to the question.** Keep what a reader needs to judge and repeat the study. Move extra tables and robustness checks to supplementary material.
6. **Write a new discussion.** Compare your result with the closest three to five studies, state limits briefly and end with the implication. Don't summarize the whole thesis.
7. **Write the abstract and title last,** then format references in the journal's style and write the cover letter, disclosing the thesis.

### Worked example: a word budget (invented)

Daniel's 82,000-word education PhD has three results chapters. He turns chapter 5, on homework time and math achievement in 1,240 students, into a paper for a journal with a 7,000-word limit.

| Section | Thesis words | Article words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction and literature | 18,000 (chapters 1 and 2) | 1,200 |
| Method | 9,500 (chapter 3) | 1,500 |
| Results | 11,000 (chapter 5) | 1,800 |
| Discussion | 6,000 (part of chapter 7) | 1,800 |
| Abstract, title, keywords | 0 | 250 |
| Limits and conclusion | 2,500 | 450 |
| **Total** | **47,000** | **7,000** |

He cuts two moderation analyses that were exploratory and moves the instrument's full item list to a supplement.

## Is publishing from your thesis self-plagiarism?

Not if you disclose it. **Text recycling is the reuse of material from an author's own earlier document in a new one, and it becomes self-plagiarism when the reuse is hidden or presents old results as new.**

Editors look at three things:

- **What is reused.** COPE's guidance for editors treats recycled methods text more leniently than recycled results or conclusions, and asks editors to judge each case [4]. <!-- VERIFY: confirm wording on the current COPE text recycling guidelines page -->
- **Whether it was disclosed.** The Text Recycling Research Project advises authors to tell editors about recycled material at submission, ideally in the cover letter, and to tell readers in a note citing the source [5].
- **Whether the results were already published.** **Redundant publication is publishing the same results in more than one journal article.** That is a different and serious problem. A thesis is not a journal article, so one article from one thesis chapter is not redundant. Two articles reporting the same chapter's results would be.

Practical rules:

1. Cite your thesis in the article, usually in the method section or a footnote.
2. Disclose it in the cover letter: "This manuscript is based on chapter 5 of my doctoral thesis (University of X, 2026), available in the university repository."
3. Rewrite rather than paste. It shortens the text anyway.
4. If the journal runs a similarity check, the thesis may show up as a match. Your disclosure explains it.

## Should your supervisor be a co-author?

Only if they meet your field's authorship rules. Under the ICMJE criteria, every author must (1) contribute substantially to the conception, design, data or analysis, (2) draft or critically revise the work, (3) approve the final version and (4) agree to be accountable for it. People who meet fewer than all four should be acknowledged instead [6].

In many lab sciences a supervisor who designed the study and revises the paper is a co-author, often last. In some humanities fields, sole-authored articles from a thesis are the norm. Agree who is an author, and in which order, in writing before you start drafting. If you published a thesis by publication, your co-authors are usually fixed already.

## Common mistakes when turning a thesis into an article

- **Keeping the thesis structure:** a 3,000-word literature review in front of a short results section.
- **Reporting every analysis.** Reviewers read this as a lack of focus.
- **Defensive writing.** Thesis prose hedges everything for examiners. Articles state the finding, then the limits.
- **Ignoring newer work.** Your literature review may be two years old. Search again before you submit.
- **Choosing the journal last.** Rewriting for a second journal's format costs weeks.

## FAQ

### Can I publish my thesis as a journal article?

Usually yes, but not as it stands. Most publishers do not treat a thesis as prior publication: Elsevier says so explicitly, and Nature has said a doctoral thesis does not count as previously published. You still need to rewrite the work as a shorter article with one clear question, tell the editor it comes from your thesis, cite the thesis, and check the target journal's own policy.

### Is publishing from a thesis self-plagiarism?

Not if you are open about it. Reusing your own thesis in an article is generally accepted, because a thesis is not treated as a formal publication. Problems arise when you hide the overlap or publish the same results twice in two journals. Disclose the thesis in your cover letter, cite it in the article, and rewrite rather than paste whole sections.

### Should my supervisor be a co-author on my thesis paper?

Only if they meet your field's authorship criteria. Under the ICMJE criteria, used widely in medicine and beyond, each author must contribute substantially to the design, data or analysis, draft or critically revise the paper, approve the final version and accept accountability. Supervisors who shaped the study and revise the manuscript usually qualify. Agree authorship and order in writing before you start drafting.

### How long does it take to turn a thesis chapter into an article?

Plan four to eight weeks of part-time work for a first submission-ready draft, then a round of supervisor comments. The slow parts are cutting the literature review, re-running or reshaping analyses for one question, and meeting the journal's format. Reformatting references is fast with a reference manager; deciding what the article's single claim is takes longest.

### How many articles can I get from one thesis?

It depends on how many distinct questions the thesis answers. A thesis by publication may already be three or four papers. A monograph thesis often yields two or three: one per results chapter, sometimes plus a review or methods paper. Each article must report something new; splitting one result across several papers is salami slicing and editors reject it.
