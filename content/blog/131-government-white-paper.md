# Government white paper: what it is and how to write a policy one

*Last updated: 13 November 2026*

A government white paper is an official document that sets out a government's policy on an issue and the changes it plans to make, often ahead of new legislation. In parliamentary systems it usually follows a green paper, which consults on options. A policy white paper, written by a government or by a think tank, NGO or industry body, follows the same logic: the problem, the evidence, the options, firm proposals and how to put them into practice.

This guide is for policy officials, think tank and NGO staff, advocates, researchers and industry associations. It explains what government white papers are, then shows how to write a policy white paper of your own. It is part of our series on white papers; for the basics, see [what is a white paper](/blog/what-is-a-white-paper).

## What is a government white paper?

**A government white paper is an official statement of policy on a subject, setting out the problem, the government's proposals and how it intends to carry them out.** It tells parliament, the public and the people affected what the government plans to do and why.

A government white paper usually:

- **States a policy position,** not just options.
- **Sets out the evidence** for change: data, research, the results of consultation.
- **Describes the proposals,** often with the legislation, funding or institutional changes needed.
- **Explains implementation:** timelines, who is responsible, how progress will be measured.
- **Comes before a bill** in many cases, so the proposals can be debated before law is drafted.

The name is said to come from the colour of the covers of shorter official papers, in contrast to the longer "blue books". <!-- VERIFY: origin of the term; cite the UK Parliament glossary or a reliable history before stating it as fact --> Today the term is used for official policy documents in many countries, and more widely for any authoritative report that argues a position.

## White paper vs green paper

Countries with a parliamentary tradition, including the UK and several Commonwealth countries, often use both terms. <!-- VERIFY: confirm current usage in the UK Parliament glossary; list other countries only after checking -->

| | Green paper | White paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Consult: test ideas and gather views | Decide: set out policy and planned changes |
| **Tone** | Open, exploratory, many options | Firm, specific proposals |
| **Typical content** | The problem, possible approaches, consultation questions | The problem, evidence, chosen proposals, implementation |
| **What usually follows** | A consultation response, then a white paper | A bill or other formal action |

The line is not strict. Some white papers still invite views on how proposals should work, and some governments publish consultation documents under other names. The European Commission also uses both terms: its green papers open a debate, and its white papers set out proposals for EU action. <!-- VERIFY: check the European Commission's own definitions page before publishing -->

## What is a white paper in parliament?

In a parliamentary system, a white paper is a government document presented to parliament that sets out policy and, often, planned legislation. It is usually published by the department responsible for the area. It is not law: any bill that follows still goes through the normal legislative stages, and members can debate and amend it. Parliamentary libraries and committees often publish briefings that summarise and scrutinise major white papers. <!-- VERIFY: check the exact procedure terms ("laid before", "presented to", "Command paper") for any parliament named -->

## Defence, foreign policy and other national white papers

Many governments publish white papers on defence, foreign policy, the economy, education and other areas, sometimes on a regular cycle. Some defence ministries publish an annual white paper describing the security environment and their defence posture; others publish one when strategy changes. <!-- VERIFY: if naming specific countries' defence or foreign policy white papers, check each title and year on the official site --> These documents are written for several readers at once: parliament, the public, allies and other governments. That makes them more careful and more general than a policy white paper from an advocacy group.

The place does not change the writing problem. Whether you are a ministry official, a city government, a think tank or an NGO, you are making the case for a policy to readers who must decide whether to support it.

## Who writes policy white papers

| Author | Purpose | Typical reader |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Government department** | State the government's policy and plans | Parliament, the public, affected sectors |
| **Agency or regulator** | Explain a proposed rule or approach and its evidence | Regulated organisations, ministers |
| **Think tank or research institute** | Propose a policy, with research behind it | Officials, legislators, journalists |
| **NGO or advocacy group** | Argue for change on behalf of a group or cause | Legislators, officials, funders, the public |
| **Industry body** | Propose rules or standards for a sector | Regulators, ministers, members |
| **University or research consortium** | Turn research into policy options | Officials and funders |

A policy white paper is longer and more detailed than a policy brief. A brief is 2 to 4 pages and usually makes one recommendation; a white paper sets out the full case, weighs several options and explains implementation. ## Structure of a policy white paper

This is the structure the White paper module in AI for Research uses for its "Policy or government white paper" kind. It works for both government and outside authors.

| Section | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| **Summary** | The problem, the main proposals and what they will achieve, in one to two pages |
| **Introduction** | The purpose of the paper, who it is for, and its scope |
| **The case for change** | The problem and its causes, with cited evidence and data |
| **Objectives** | What the policy should achieve, in measurable terms where possible |
| **Proposals** | Numbered proposals, each with its rationale, expected effect, cost and who must act |
| **Options considered** | A table of options, their benefits, costs and risks, the strength of evidence, and why each was chosen or not |
| **Implementation and timeline** | Phases, responsibilities, legislation needed, funding |
| **Impact** | Benefits, costs, effects on different groups, risks |
| **Consultation questions** | Numbered questions for readers to respond to, if the paper seeks views |
| **Next steps** | What happens after publication |
| **References and annexes** | Sources, data tables, the consultation summary |

### The options table

The options table is the heart of a credible policy white paper. It shows readers that you considered alternatives fairly before choosing.

| Option | Expected benefits | Costs and risks | Strength of evidence | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Do nothing | [baseline] | [cost of the problem continuing] | — | Rejected: [reason] |
| Option A | [benefit, cited] | [cost, cited or estimated] | Moderate | Proposed |
| Option B | [benefit, cited] | [cost] | Limited | Rejected: [reason] |
| Option C | [benefit] | [cost] | Strong, but from other settings | Pilot first |

Always include "do nothing" as an option. It forces you to state the cost of the problem.

### Writing proposals that can be acted on

Each proposal should answer five questions:

1. **What** exactly will change?
2. **Who** must act: which ministry, agency, legislature or organisation?
3. **How much** will it cost, and who pays?
4. **When:** by what date or in what phase?
5. **How will we know** it worked: what indicator, measured how?

"Improve access to mental health services" is an aim. "Fund [number] school-based counsellors in [area] from [year], measured by [indicator]" is a proposal.

## How to write a policy white paper, step by step

1. **Define the decision and the reader.** Which decision maker do you want to act, and what do they already believe? A paper for a legislature's committee differs from one for a ministry.
2. **Gather the evidence.** Start with existing reviews and official data; add research on what has worked elsewhere, and note how well it transfers. See [how to find sources for a research paper](/blog/how-to-find-sources-for-a-research-paper) and [levels of evidence and study types](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types).
3. **Talk to the people affected.** Consultation, interviews or survey data make proposals more credible and catch practical problems early.
4. **Set out and test the options.** Build the options table before you write prose. Ask someone who disagrees with you to fill in the costs and risks column.
5. **Write the proposals,** each answering the five questions above.
6. **Draft the summary last,** for a reader who will read nothing else.
7. **Check every number and claim** against its source. See [how to check a claim against the research](/blog/how-to-check-a-claim-against-the-research).
8. **Review for fairness and clarity:** a policy expert, a subject expert, and someone from the group most affected.

### Style

- **Plain language.** Officials and legislators read many documents; short sentences and clear headings help yours get read.
- **Evidence before argument.** State what the evidence shows, then what you propose.
- **Say how transferable evidence is.** Research from one country, city or system may not apply to another; say so.
- **Neutral tone,** even in advocacy. Overstatement is easy for opponents to quote.
- **Declare who wrote and funded it,** on the first page.

## Policy white paper example: an outline

*Example (invented for illustration).* A public health NGO proposes changes to school meals.

```text
Title: Feeding every child: the case for universal school meals in [area]
Subtitle: Evidence, options and a phased plan

Summary (two pages)
1. Introduction: purpose, audience (education committee, ministry), scope
2. The case for change: [current coverage], [food insecurity data, cited],
   research on meals and attendance and learning (cited, with strength)
3. Objectives: [coverage target], [attendance indicator]
4. Proposals
   4.1 Universal meals for primary schools from [year]
   4.2 Nutrition standards set by [body]
   4.3 Local sourcing pilot in [number] districts
5. Options considered (table: do nothing, means-tested expansion,
   universal primary, universal all ages)
6. Implementation and timeline: phases, responsibilities, funding sources
7. Impact: benefits, costs [placeholder until costed], effects by group
8. Consultation questions (6 numbered questions)
9. Next steps
References · Annex: data tables
```

Values in brackets would come from official data and the NGO's own costings. ## FAQ

### What is a government white paper?

A government white paper is an official document that sets out a government's policy on an issue and the changes it intends to make, often before it brings in new laws. It explains the problem, the evidence, the proposals and how they will be put into practice. In parliamentary systems it usually comes after a consultation and before a bill.

### What is the difference between a white paper and a green paper?

In parliamentary systems that use both terms, a green paper is a consultation document: it sets out ideas or options and asks for views. A white paper sets out firmer policy proposals the government intends to adopt. The line is not strict; some white papers still invite comment on details, and some governments use other names.

### What is a white paper in parliament?

In parliament, a white paper is a document a government presents to set out its policy and planned legislation on a subject. It is usually published by the department responsible and laid before or presented to parliament. It is not law itself; any new law still has to pass through the normal legislative process.

### What is a policy white paper?

A policy white paper is any white paper that argues for a policy. Governments publish them to state their own plans; think tanks, NGOs, industry bodies and researchers publish them to propose policies to government. Both kinds set out the problem, the evidence, the options considered and specific proposals.

### How long is a government white paper?

There is no fixed length. Short policy white papers from think tanks and NGOs often run 10 to 30 pages. Government white papers on major reforms can run to well over 100 pages with annexes. Every one should have a summary short enough to read in a few minutes.

### Can AI help write a policy white paper?

AI can draft structure, summarise research you supply, lay out options in a table and tighten language. It must not supply the evidence or the local facts. Use a tool that cites real sources, give it your own data and current policy details, and have policy and subject experts check every claim before publication.
