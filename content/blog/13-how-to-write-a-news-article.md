# How to write a news article: format, structure and example

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

To write a news article, find what is new and why it matters, gather documents and at least two sources, then write a one-sentence lead with the core fact. Order the body by importance, attribute every fact with "said," check every name and number, and add a headline, byline and dateline. The 8 steps below show how.

This guide is part of our [AP style guide](/blog/ap-style-guide). It ends with a complete example article, annotated paragraph by paragraph.

## The 8 steps at a glance

| Step | What you do | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Find the angle | Decide what is new and who it affects | One sentence: "The story is that..." |
| 2. Research | Pull documents, data, studies, past coverage | A source list with links |
| 3. Interview | Talk to the people involved and affected; record with permission | Notes or a transcript |
| 4. Write the lead | One sentence with the core news | 25 to 35 words |
| 5. Structure the body | Order paragraphs by importance | An inverted pyramid draft |
| 6. Quotes and attribution | Choose the best quotes; attribute every fact | Clean AP attribution |
| 7. Fact-check | Check every name, title, number, date and quote | A checked draft |
| 8. Headline, dateline, byline | Package the story | A filed story |

## Step 1: find the angle (news values)

A topic is not a story. "The school budget" is a topic. "The school board voted to close two schools to cover a $3.1 million gap" is a story. The angle is what happened, to whom, and why a reader should care now.

Editors judge stories on news values. Most journalism courses teach a version of this list:

| News value | Question it asks | Example angle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timeliness | Did it just happen, or is it about to? | A vote tonight |
| Impact | How many people does it affect, and how much? | 600 students change schools |
| Proximity | Is it close to our readers? | A local plant, not a national trend |
| Prominence | Is a well-known person or institution involved? | The mayor, the state university |
| Conflict | Is there a dispute or a decision with sides? | Parents vs. the board |
| Novelty | Is it unusual? | First closure in 15 years |
| Human interest | Does a person's experience make it real? | A family with three children at a closing school |

Write your angle as one sentence before you research. It will change, but it keeps you from collecting everything.

## Step 2: research and sources

Start with what is written down; documents are harder to spin than people.

- **Documents and data:** agendas, minutes, budgets, court filings, inspection records, the agency's own reports.
- **Studies:** for health, science or policy, find the actual paper, not the press release about it, and check whether it is peer-reviewed, a preprint or retracted.
- **People:** the decision-maker, the people affected, an independent expert, and anyone the story criticizes.

Keep a source list as you go (each document with its link, each person with title and phone). You will need it in step 7.

## Step 3: interview and record

Prepare five to eight open questions ("How did the board reach this number?"). Ask for specifics: dates, amounts, documents you can see. Save the hard question for near the end, close with "Who else should I talk to?", and confirm the spelling of every name and the exact title.

**Recording.** Record if you can, with permission. Federal law and most states allow recording when one party to the conversation consents, but about a dozen states require the consent of everyone in the conversation, according to the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press. [1] The safe habit is to ask on the recording: "Is it OK if I record this?" Our [interview transcription guide](/blog/interview-transcription) covers consent rules and tools, and [from transcript to story](/blog/transcript-to-story) covers turning a recording into quotes.

## Step 4: write the lead

The lead is the first sentence of a news story. It carries the single most important fact, plus as many of who, what, when and where as fit cleanly.

Test your lead with three questions:

1. Would a reader who stops here know what happened?
2. Does it lead with the action, not the time, place or meeting?
3. Is it under about 35 words?

| Weak lead | Strong lead |
| --- | --- |
| At its regular Tuesday meeting, the Brennan County school board discussed the budget. | The Brennan County school board voted 5-2 Tuesday to close two elementary schools next June. |
| A new study has some interesting findings about sleep. | Teenagers who started school after 8:30 a.m. slept about 30 minutes more a night, a new study of 2,000 students found. |

(Both are fictional examples.)

Put the day of the week in the lead for events within the past week ("Tuesday"), not "yesterday." AP style uses the day of the week for dates within seven days and the date for anything further away.

## Step 5: structure the body (inverted pyramid)

Most news stories use the inverted pyramid, a structure that puts the most important facts first and orders everything after it by falling importance. After the lead come the why, the best quote, supporting facts, the other side, what happens next, and finally background that is safe to cut. Keep paragraphs to one to three sentences; the annotated example below shows the order. Our full guide to the [inverted pyramid](/blog/inverted-pyramid) has a diagram, history and template.

## Step 6: quotes and attribution (AP rules)

Attribution tells the reader where each fact came from. In a news article, every fact that is not common knowledge or something you saw yourself needs a source.

**The AP basics:**

| Rule | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use "said," past tense | Okafor claimed the plan saves money. | Okafor said the plan saves money. |
| Name before the verb | ..., said Okafor. | ..., Okafor said. |
| Verb first when a long title follows | ..., Maria Okafor, chair of the Brennan County school board, said. | ..., said Maria Okafor, chair of the Brennan County school board. |
| Full name and title on first reference, last name after | Maria said... | First: board chair Maria Okafor. Then: Okafor. |
| Attribute once per quote, not per sentence | "We tried," she said. "We failed," she said. | "We tried. We failed," she said. |
| Comma and period go inside the quote marks | "We had no choice", she said. | "We had no choice," she said. |
| Never alter a quote's words | Fix the speaker's grammar inside the quote marks. | Quote exactly, or paraphrase without quote marks. |

**What to quote.** Quote opinion, emotion and memorable phrasing. Paraphrase facts and numbers, which you can state more clearly yourself. "The district will save $1.4 million a year" is a paraphrase with attribution; "No one on this board wanted to do this" is a quote.

**Partial quotes and ellipses.** Use partial quotes sparingly, and never use an ellipsis to remove words that change the meaning.

Check the current AP Stylebook for edge cases; it is updated every year. [2]

## Step 7: fact-check

Before you file, check every claim against your source list: names and titles; every number against the original document (recompute percentages yourself); dates and days of the week; quotes against the recording, word for word; studies (the paper says what you say, and is not retracted); and fairness (anyone criticized had a real chance to respond, high in the story).

For a full process, see our editor's checklist on [how to fact-check an article](/blog/how-to-fact-check-an-article).

## Step 8: headline, dateline, byline

**Headline.** Subject, active verb, key fact, present tense, sentence case in AP style: "School board votes to close two elementary schools." Our guide to [how to write a headline](/blog/how-to-write-a-headline) covers formulas and SEO.

**Byline.** Your name, and your title or outlet if required: "By Jordan Lee, Staff Writer."

**Dateline.** A dateline is the place name at the start of a story that tells readers where the reporting happened. In AP style:

| Rule | Example |
| --- | --- |
| City in capital letters, then the state in AP abbreviation, then a dash | BRENNAN FALLS, Ore. — |
| AP state abbreviations are not postal codes | Calif., not CA; Mass., not MA |
| Some states are never abbreviated (Alaska, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Maine, Ohio, Texas, Utah) | AKRON, Ohio — |
| Major cities on AP's standalone list take no state | CHICAGO — / BOSTON — |
| Use a dateline only when you reported from that place | No dateline for a story written from your desk about a distant event |

In the body text, AP has spelled out state names since 2014. [3]

## Complete example article with notes

The article below is a fictional example. Brennan County, its schools and every person, number and quote are invented.

```
School board votes to close two elementary schools

By Jordan Lee, Staff Writer

BRENNAN FALLS, Ore. — The Brennan County school board voted 5-2 Tuesday to
close two elementary schools next June, moving about 600 students to three
other buildings.

The board said falling enrollment and a $3.1 million budget gap left it no
choice. District enrollment has dropped 18% since 2016, according to a
district report presented at the meeting.

"No one on this board wanted to do this," board chair Maria Okafor said.
"But we can't keep paying to heat half-empty buildings while we cut
teachers."

Lincoln and Westview elementary schools will close. Their students will move
to Ridge, Oakmont and Fairview elementary schools, which the district says
have room for about 750 more students.

The district expects to save about $1.4 million a year, mostly in building
and staff costs, the report says.

About 150 parents attended, and 22 spoke against the plan. Several said the
change would add 20 minutes or more to their children's bus rides.

"My daughter already leaves at 7:05," said Angela Price, whose two children
attend Westview. "Now she'll leave in the dark."

Board member Dan Reyes, who voted no, said the district had not studied bus
routes. "We are voting on a map nobody has driven," he said.

Superintendent Carla Mendes said Wednesday that the district will publish
new bus routes by March 1 and hold meetings for affected families in
January.

Lincoln Elementary opened in 1954. Westview opened in 1971. The board last
closed a school in 2009.
```

**Paragraph by paragraph:**

| Para | Job | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | Subject, verb, fact | Present tense, sentence case, no period |
| Dateline | Where the reporting happened | City in caps; "Ore." is AP's abbreviation |
| 1 | Lead | Decision, vote count, day, scale; 30 words |
| 2 | Why, with evidence | The number names its source |
| 3 | Best quote | From the decision-maker; adds feeling, not facts |
| 4 to 5 | Supporting facts | Paraphrased with attribution |
| 6 to 7 | Affected people | A count first, then one human voice with identification |
| 8 | Dissent | A named board member, high enough not to be cut |
| 9 | What happens next | New source, new day ("Wednesday"), concrete dates |
| 10 | Tail | Background; an editor can cut it |

## FAQ

### What is the format of a news article?

A news article has a headline, a byline, a dateline if it was reported outside your home area, a one-sentence lead with the core news, and a body ordered by importance: the why, the best quote, supporting facts, the other side, what happens next and background. Paragraphs are short, usually one to three sentences, and every fact is attributed.

### How long should a news article be?

Length follows the news. A brief can be 100 to 200 words, a typical local news story 400 to 800, and an important story with several sources 1,000 or more. Write it tight, then let the editor cut from the bottom. If you can say it in 500 words, a 900-word version is padding.

### How do you write a dateline in AP style?

Write the city in capital letters, followed by the state in AP's abbreviation (not the postal code), then a dash: BRENNAN FALLS, Ore. — Large cities on AP's standalone list, such as CHICAGO or BOSTON, appear without a state. Use a dateline only when you report the story from that place.

### Should a news article use said or says?

Use said. News stories report what happened, so attribution is in the past tense, and AP prefers said over loaded verbs such as claimed, admitted or stated. Put the name before the verb (Okafor said) unless a long title follows the name, in which case the verb can come first.

### How do students write a news article?

Students should follow the same 8 steps as working reporters: pick an angle with clear news value, gather documents and at least two human sources, record interviews with permission, write a one-sentence lead, order the body by importance, attribute every fact, check every name and number, and add a headline. School stories still need named sources.
