# How to write a research report: outline, structure and example

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

A research report outline has eight sections: title, executive summary, background, method, findings, discussion, recommendations and references. Write the executive summary last, put the answer in its first two sentences, and make every finding a sentence that states a result. The fill-in template below gives you all eight with prompts.

This guide is for reports written for organizations: market research, policy analysis, program evaluations, technical studies, NGO reports. It is part of our [research and citations series](/blog/ai-citation-generator).

**A research report is a document that sets out a question, how it was investigated, what was found and what it means, for readers who need to act on it.** The last part is what separates a professional report from a school paper. Your reader has a decision to make. Every section should help them make it.

## The 8-section research report outline

| # | Section | What it answers | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title | What is this about, and for whom? | One line, plus subtitle |
| 2 | Executive summary | What did you find and what should I do? | One page maximum |
| 3 | Background | Why does this question matter now? | Half a page to two pages |
| 4 | Method | How did you find out, and how far can I trust it? | Half a page to two pages |
| 5 | Findings | What did you find? | The longest section |
| 6 | Discussion | What does it mean, and what are the limits? | One to three pages |
| 7 | Recommendations | What should we do? | One page |
| 8 | References | Where can I check this? | As needed |

Appendices are optional: full data tables, survey instruments, interview guides, search logs.

Write in a different order than you present: method, findings, discussion, recommendations, background, executive summary, title.

## Fill-in research report template

Copy this into your editor and replace each bracket. The prompts in italics are there to delete.

```markdown
# [Title: the finding or the question, in plain words]
## [Subtitle: report type, organization, date]

Prepared for: [reader or team]   Prepared by: [names]   Date: [date]

## Executive summary
*Two sentences: the answer to the question and your confidence in it.*
[Answer.]
*Three to five bullets: the key findings, each with a number.*
- [Finding 1]
- [Finding 2]
- [Finding 3]
*One to three bullets: what you recommend, and by when.*
- [Recommendation]

## 1. Background
*Why this question, why now. What the reader already knows. What decision this report informs.*
[Context.]
**Question:** [The one question this report answers.]

## 2. Method
*What you did, so a skeptic can judge it.*
- Data sources: [surveys, interviews, internal data, published studies]
- Sample: [who, how many, when, how chosen]
- Analysis: [what you compared or measured]
- Limits: [what this method can't tell you]

## 3. Findings
*One subsection per finding. The heading is the finding as a sentence.*
### 3.1 [Finding as a full sentence, with the number]
[Evidence: chart or table, then two to four sentences explaining it.]
### 3.2 [Finding as a full sentence]
[Evidence.]

## 4. Discussion
*What the findings mean together. What they don't show. How they compare with other research.*
[Interpretation.]
**Limitations:** [What could change the conclusion.]

## 5. Recommendations
*Numbered. Each one: action, owner, timing, and the finding it rests on.*
1. [Action]. Owner: [team], by [date]. Based on finding [3.x].
2. [Action]

## References
[Formatted in one style, e.g. APA 7.]

## Appendices
A. [Data tables]  B. [Instruments]  C. [Search log]
```

## How to write an executive summary decision-makers read

**An executive summary is a one-page version of the report that gives the answer, the key evidence and the recommendation, so a reader who reads nothing else can act.** Many of your readers will read nothing else.

Rules:

1. **Answer first.** The first sentence states the conclusion. Not the purpose of the report.
2. **Numbers, not adjectives.** "Turnover fell by five points" instead of "turnover improved significantly."
3. **Match the report.** Every claim in the summary appears in the findings. Nothing new.
4. **Say how sure you are.** "Strong evidence from 12 months of data" or "early signal from a small pilot."
5. **End with the ask.** What you want the reader to approve, fund or stop.

**Wrong:** "This report examines the evening-hours pilot conducted at three branches and explores its implications for future service planning."
**Right:** "Opening three branches until 9 p.m. raised weekday visits by 18% at a cost of about $4 per added visit. We recommend extending evening hours to two more branches in 2027."

## Presenting findings: charts, tables and the "so what"

Each finding needs three parts: the claim, the evidence and the meaning.

- **Headline the finding.** Use the finding as the heading: "Evening visitors are younger than daytime visitors," not "Visitor demographics."
- **One chart, one point.** If a chart needs a paragraph to explain, split it or use a table.
- **Tables for exact values, charts for patterns.** Readers comparing specific figures want a table.
- **Label the source and the base** under every chart: "Source: door counts, Oct 2025 to Mar 2026; n = 3 branches."
- **Write the "so what" sentence.** After the evidence, say what it means for the decision: "That means extra hours reach a group the library currently misses."

## Citing sources in a professional report

Professional reports cite to let readers check, not to show effort.

- **Pick one style and use it throughout.** If your organization has none, APA 7 handles reports from agencies and organizations well. [1]
- **Cite in the sentence where it matters:** "According to the Census Bureau's 2024 estimates…" Then add the full reference at the end.
- **Cite primary sources.** The study, not the news story about it. The dataset, not a chart someone made from it.
- **Link and list.** Hyperlinks help on screen; the reference list survives printing.
- **Check before you send.** Every DOI resolves, every link opens, nothing is retracted.

## Annotated example research report (short)

*This is a fictional example. The library, figures and people are invented to show the structure.*

```markdown
# Evening hours brought in new library users at a low cost per visit
## Pilot evaluation, Riverbend Public Library (fictional), April 2026

## Executive summary
Opening three branches until 9 p.m. on weekdays raised weekday visits by 18%
over six months, at about $4 per added visit. Most evening visitors were
aged 18 to 34, a group that makes up a small share of daytime visits.
- Weekday visits rose from 41,000 to 48,400 (Oct 2025 to Mar 2026 vs. the
  same months a year earlier).
- 62% of evening visitors surveyed were 18 to 34, vs. 21% of daytime visitors.
- Added staff cost was $29,600, or about $4 per added visit.
Recommendation: extend evening hours to two more branches from January 2027
and repeat this evaluation after six months.

## 1. Background
The board asked whether longer hours would reach residents who work days.
Question: Did the evening-hours pilot increase visits enough to justify its cost?

## 2. Method
- Data: automatic door counts; a two-week visitor survey (n = 412); payroll.
- Comparison: same six months in the previous year at the same branches.
- Limits: no control branches; the survey covered two weeks only.

## 3. Findings
### 3.1 Weekday visits rose 18% at pilot branches
[Table: monthly visits, pilot year vs. previous year]
### 3.2 Evening visitors were mostly 18 to 34
[Chart: age group by time of visit]
### 3.3 Each added visit cost about $4 in staff time

## 4. Discussion
The rise is unlikely to be seasonal because the comparison uses the same
months. Without control branches, we can't rule out a citywide increase.
Limitations: ...

## 5. Recommendations
1. Extend evening hours to Eastside and Mill Road branches from Jan 2027.
   Owner: Branch services. Based on 3.1 and 3.3.
2. Add control branches to the next evaluation. Owner: Research team.

## References
...
```

What makes it work:

| Element | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| Title states the finding | A reader skimming a list of reports knows the result |
| First sentence of the summary is the answer, with numbers | The board can decide from one paragraph |
| Method states its limits up front | The reader trusts the findings more, not less |
| Finding headings are full sentences | The table of contents reads as a summary |
| Recommendations have owners and dates | Someone can act on Monday |

## How to write a research report for business problems

Business reports add two pressures: a decision deadline and a reader who may not share your vocabulary. Adjust the template:

- **Start the background with the business problem** in the reader's terms: cost, risk, revenue, customers.
- **Give options, not just one recommendation.** Two or three, with cost and risk for each, then your pick.
- **Translate effects into money or time** wherever you honestly can, and show the math in an appendix.
- **Put methods detail in an appendix** and keep a three-line method summary in the body.

## Writing a research report with AI: what to delegate, what to check

An **AI research report writer** is useful for some parts of the job and risky for others.

| Task | Delegate to AI? | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Turning your notes into an outline | Yes | That the question is yours |
| First draft of background | Yes, with sources | Every citation opens and supports the sentence |
| First draft of method | Yes, from your notes | Sample sizes and dates match your records |
| Findings | Draft only, from your data | Every number against the source table |
| Discussion and limits | Prompt it for limits you missed | That it doesn't overstate certainty |
| Recommendations | No; write these yourself | |
| Executive summary | Draft after the report is done | Nothing appears that isn't in the findings |
| Proofreading and tone | Yes | Technical terms weren't "simplified" into errors |

The rule: AI can draft words, but the facts come from your data and your sources. A chatbot that writes a background section from memory can attach a real-sounding citation to a paper that doesn't exist. Use a tool that cites from a real index, and check.

## FAQ

### What are the main parts of a research report?

A professional research report has eight parts: title, executive summary, background, method, findings, discussion, recommendations and references. Appendices are optional and hold detail most readers skip, such as full data tables or interview guides. The executive summary comes first but is written last, after you know what the findings mean.

### How long should a research report be?

As long as the decision needs and no longer. Many internal reports run 5 to 20 pages plus appendices. The executive summary should fit on one page regardless of total length. If a section doesn't change what the reader will decide or do, move it to an appendix or cut it.

### How do I write a research report outline?

Start from the decision the reader has to make and write it as one question. Then list the eight sections and, under each, two or three bullet points of what it must say. Write the findings bullets as full sentences that state a result. If you can't, you aren't ready to draft that section.

### Can AI write a research report?

AI can draft sections, tighten language and summarize sources, but it can't be the author. It may misstate numbers or cite sources that don't support the sentence. Use it for structure, first drafts of background and method, and editing. Check every figure and citation against the source, and write the findings and recommendations yourself.

### What citation style should a business research report use?

Use the style your organization or client already uses. If there is none, APA 7 works well for reports because it is author-date and handles reports by agencies and organizations cleanly. Whatever you choose, name sources in the text, link where readers read on screen, and include a full reference list.
