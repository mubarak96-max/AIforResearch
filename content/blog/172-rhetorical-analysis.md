# Rhetorical analysis: how to analyze ethos, logos, pathos and kairos

*Last updated: 14 November 2026*

A rhetorical analysis explains how a text tries to persuade its audience. To write one, identify the rhetorical situation (speaker, audience, occasion, purpose), find the choices the writer makes, including appeals to ethos, logos and pathos, timing (kairos), structure and word choice, and argue how those choices serve the purpose. You analyze the strategy, not whether you agree with the argument.

This guide is for high school students preparing for AP English Language, and for college students in writing and communication courses. It includes a worked analysis of Lincoln's Gettysburg Address. It belongs to our series on essays and sources, whose hub is [what is a thesis statement](/blog/what-is-a-thesis-statement).

## What is rhetorical analysis?

**Rhetorical analysis is the study of how a text persuades: what the writer wants, who they are addressing, and which choices they make to get there.** Aristotle defined rhetoric as the ability to see, in each case, the available means of persuasion [1]. A rhetorical analysis works backward from a finished text to those means.

It is not a summary and not a review. Compare three sentences about the same speech:

| Type | Sentence (illustrative) |
| --- | --- |
| Summary | Lincoln says the soldiers died so the nation could live. |
| Opinion | Lincoln's speech is one of the best ever written. |
| Analysis | Lincoln turns a cemetery dedication into a call to finish the war, by shifting the speech's focus from the dead to "us the living". |

Only the third explains how the text works.

## What are ethos, logos and pathos?

**They are Aristotle's three modes of persuasion: through the speaker's character (ethos), the audience's emotions (pathos) and the argument itself (logos)** [1].

| Appeal | It persuades through | Look for | Ask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ethos** | The speaker's credibility and character | Credentials, shared values, fairness to opponents, humility, "we" | Why should this audience trust this speaker? |
| **Pathos** | Emotions in the audience | Vivid images, stories, loaded words, appeals to fear, pride, grief, hope | What should the audience feel, and why does that help the purpose? |
| **Logos** | Reasoning and evidence | Claims, reasons, data, examples, cause and effect, contrasts | Does the reasoning hold, and is the evidence fitting for this audience? |

Two cautions. First, the appeals overlap: a statistic about deaths carries pathos as well as logos. Name the dominant appeal and say how the others support it. Second, "the author uses pathos" is not analysis. Say which words create which feeling, and why that feeling serves the purpose.

## What is kairos?

**Kairos is the right moment: the sense that a text fits its time, place and occasion.** It is a Greek term for timeliness that rhetoric teachers add to the three appeals. <!-- VERIFY: add a scholarly source on kairos in classical rhetoric if one is wanted; the article currently relies on the general definition --> A speech has strong kairos when the occasion gives its words extra force: a memorial, an election, a disaster, an anniversary.

Ask: why this text now? What did the audience already know, fear or expect at that moment? Would it work as well a year earlier or later?

## What is the rhetorical situation?

**The rhetorical situation is the context that calls a text into being: in Lloyd Bitzer's account, an exigence (a problem that discourse can change), an audience able to act on it, and constraints that shape what can be said** [2].

| Bitzer's element | Meaning | Gettysburg Address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exigence | An imperfection that speech can help change | A divided nation in the middle of a war, with heavy losses at Gettysburg |
| Audience | People who can be moved to act | The crowd at the cemetery, and the Union public who would read the speech in newspapers |
| Constraints | Beliefs, facts, traditions and the speaker's position that limit or help the speech | A dedication ceremony with a main oration by Edward Everett; Lincoln's role as president; the conventions of a funeral address |

Purdue OWL describes the rhetorical situation with similar elements, including the author, audience, purpose and setting [3]. Use whichever model your instructor prefers; the point is to analyze the text in its context.

## What is SOAPSTone?

**SOAPSTone is a checklist for the rhetorical situation: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject and Tone.** It is widely used in AP English classes as a pre-reading step. <!-- VERIFY: origin of SOAPSTone (often attributed to Tommy Boley and College Board teacher training) before naming anyone -->

| Letter | Question | Gettysburg Address |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S**peaker | Who is speaking, and what do we know about them? | Abraham Lincoln, president of the United States |
| **O**ccasion | When, where, in response to what? | 19 November 1863, dedication of the Soldiers' National Cemetery at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, four and a half months after the battle |
| **A**udience | Who is listening or reading? | The crowd at the dedication; the wider Union public through the press |
| **P**urpose | What does the speaker want the audience to think or do? | To honor the dead and recommit the living to winning the war and preserving the nation's founding ideals |
| **S**ubject | What is it about? | The meaning of the soldiers' sacrifice and the purpose of the war |
| **Tone** | What is the speaker's attitude? | Solemn, humble, resolute |

SOAPSTone is a planning tool. Do not structure your essay as six paragraphs, one per letter.

## How to write a rhetorical analysis in seven steps

1. **Read the text twice.** Once for what it says, once for how it says it.
2. **Map the rhetorical situation** with SOAPSTone or Bitzer's three elements.
3. **Find the purpose** in one sentence: "The speaker wants [audience] to [think or do] [something]."
4. **Mark the choices.** Underline appeals, repeated words, shifts in tone, structure (where the text turns), and sentence patterns.
5. **Group the choices** into two or three strategies that do most of the work.
6. **Write the thesis:** speaker + purpose + strategies + effect on the audience.
7. **Draft body paragraphs** that each analyze one strategy: quote, explain how it works, connect it to the purpose.

## Worked example: Lincoln's Gettysburg Address

Lincoln delivered the address on 19 November 1863 at the dedication of the national cemetery at Gettysburg. Edward Everett gave the main oration first, and it lasted about two hours; Lincoln's speech is about 270 words long [4]. Five manuscript copies survive and their wording differs slightly; quotations here follow the Bliss copy, the version most often reproduced [4]. <!-- VERIFY: quotations against the Bliss copy at the Library of Congress or Abraham Lincoln Online -->

### Strategy 1: Ethos through humility and "we"

Lincoln never uses "I". He speaks as one of "us", and he lowers his own role: "The world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here." A president claiming that his words do not matter builds credibility with a grieving audience, because it puts the soldiers above the speaker. The irony, that these lines are among the best remembered in American speech, does not weaken the move at the time it was made.

### Strategy 2: Logos through a chain of reasoning

The speech is built as an argument in three time frames: past ("Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal"), present ("Now we are engaged in a great civil war, testing whether that nation, or any nation so conceived and so dedicated, can long endure") and future ("that this nation, under God, shall have a new birth of freedom"). The logic runs: the nation was founded on a proposition; the war tests whether it can survive; therefore the living must finish the work. Lincoln also redefines the occasion. He says the living "can not dedicate", "can not consecrate", "can not hallow" this ground, because the soldiers already have. The reasoning moves the duty from the ceremony to the audience: "It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work."

### Strategy 3: Pathos through birth and death imagery

The speech opens with birth ("brought forth", "conceived") and closes with rebirth ("a new birth of freedom"). Between them sit the dead who "gave their lives that that nation might live" and "the last full measure of devotion". The imagery turns grief into purpose: the deaths become the price of the nation's renewal, which only the living can make worthwhile, "that these dead shall not have died in vain".

### Strategy 4: Kairos and brevity

A cemetery dedication, months after a battle with heavy losses and with the war's end not in sight, gave Lincoln an audience ready to ask what the deaths had been for. After a two-hour oration, a speech of about two minutes stood out. Its brevity fits the occasion's humility, and the closing triad, "government of the people, by the people, for the people", gives the audience a line short enough to carry home.

### Example thesis statements

Each thesis names the speaker, the purpose, the strategies and the effect. These are examples written for this guide.

- **Strong:** "In the Gettysburg Address, Lincoln turns a cemetery dedication into a call to finish the war, using humble, collective ethos and imagery of birth and death to move the audience's duty from honoring the dead to completing their 'unfinished work'."
- **Strong:** "Lincoln's argument in three time frames, from the founding to the war to a 'new birth of freedom', reframes the Civil War as a test of the nation's founding proposition, giving a grieving audience a reason to keep fighting."
- **Weak:** "Lincoln uses ethos, pathos and logos in the Gettysburg Address." *(Names the appeals but says nothing about purpose or effect.)*
- **Weak:** "The Gettysburg Address is a powerful and moving speech." *(An opinion, not an analysis.)*

For more on strong and weak thesis statements, see [what is a thesis statement](/blog/what-is-a-thesis-statement).

## Rhetorical analysis essay outline

1. **Introduction**
   - The rhetorical situation in one or two sentences (speaker, occasion, audience).
   - Thesis: purpose, two or three strategies, effect.
2. **Body paragraph 1: first strategy**
   - Topic sentence naming the strategy and its effect.
   - Quotation or close paraphrase.
   - Commentary: how the choice works on this audience, and how it serves the purpose.
3. **Body paragraph 2: second strategy** (same pattern)
4. **Body paragraph 3: third strategy or the text's turn** (same pattern)
5. **Conclusion**
   - What the strategies achieve together.
   - Why it mattered at that moment, or what it shows about persuasion.

Organize body paragraphs either by strategy (as above) or by the order of the text (opening, turn, close). Following the text's order works well for short speeches whose structure is part of the argument, such as the Gettysburg Address.

## Using rhetorical analysis on the AP Lang exam

**The rhetorical analysis question is the second of three free-response questions on the AP English Language and Composition exam: students analyze how a writer's language choices contribute to the purpose and meaning of a nonfiction passage** [5]. The free-response section lasts 2 hours 15 minutes, including a 15-minute reading period, and the exam is fully digital [5].

Two habits that help on timed essays:

- **Use verbs that say what the writer does**: "reframes", "concedes", "contrasts", "invites", "dismisses". "Uses" says nothing.
- **Tie every quotation to the purpose.** After each quote, write a sentence that begins "This choice..." and finishes with its effect on the audience.

The synthesis question on the same exam needs a different skill; see [how to write a synthesis essay](/blog/synthesis-essay).

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Example (illustrative) | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Summarizing | "Then Lincoln says the ground cannot be dedicated." | Say why he says it and what it does to the audience |
| Appeal spotting | "This is an example of pathos." | Name the feeling, the words that create it, and the purpose it serves |
| Arguing with the text | "Lincoln is wrong that the world will not remember." | Analyze the effect of the claim on the audience in 1863 |
| Ignoring the audience | Analysis with no mention of who is listening | Name the audience in the thesis and in each paragraph |
| Five-paragraph ethos/pathos/logos | One paragraph per appeal regardless of the text | Choose the two or three strategies this text relies on most |
| Vague verbs | "Lincoln uses words to show..." | "Lincoln reframes...", "Lincoln shifts the duty from..." |

## Rhetorical analysis checklist

1. Have you named the speaker, occasion, audience and purpose in the introduction?
2. Does the thesis state the purpose and two or three specific strategies?
3. Does each body paragraph analyze one strategy with a quotation?
4. Does every quotation have commentary that links it to the purpose?
5. Have you used precise verbs instead of "uses" and "shows"?
6. Have you avoided judging whether the argument is right?
7. Are your quotations exact, and from a named version of the text?

## FAQ

### What is a rhetorical analysis?

A rhetorical analysis is an essay that explains how a text tries to persuade its audience, and how well. Instead of agreeing or disagreeing with the writer, you identify the rhetorical situation, the writer's purpose and the choices they make, such as appeals to credibility, emotion and logic, structure and word choice, and you argue how those choices serve the purpose for that audience.

### What are ethos, pathos and logos?

They are the three modes of persuasion Aristotle describes in the Rhetoric. Ethos is persuasion through the speaker's character and credibility. Pathos is persuasion through the emotions the speech stirs in the audience. Logos is persuasion through the argument itself: reasons, evidence and logic. Most strong texts combine all three, and a good analysis shows how they work together.

### What is kairos in rhetoric?

Kairos is the right or opportune moment, and the sense of fitness to that moment. A speech uses kairos when it draws its force from the timing and the occasion, such as a dedication, a crisis or an anniversary. In an analysis, ask why this text was effective at this time, for this audience, and whether it would work the same way on another day.

### How do you start a rhetorical analysis essay?

Open with the rhetorical situation in one or two sentences: who wrote the text, when, for which audience and in response to what. Then state your thesis, which names the writer's purpose and the main choices they use to achieve it. Avoid opening with a dictionary definition of rhetoric or a summary of the whole text.

### What is SOAPSTone?

SOAPSTone is a reading checklist for the rhetorical situation: Speaker, Occasion, Audience, Purpose, Subject and Tone. You fill in each element before you analyze a text, so your essay rests on who is speaking to whom and why. It is widely taught in AP English classes as a first step, not as the structure for the essay itself.

### What is the difference between rhetorical analysis and literary analysis?

A literary analysis interprets the meaning of a work of fiction, poetry or drama, often through themes and imagery. A rhetorical analysis examines how a nonfiction text, such as a speech, essay, letter or advertisement, tries to persuade a specific audience at a specific moment. The first asks what the text means; the second asks how it works on its readers.
