# From transcript to story: how to find, quote and check interview material

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

Once you transcribe an interview, the real work starts. Follow five steps: organize your transcripts, verify the passages you might use against the audio, find the material that answers your question, choose quotes and attribute them in AP style, then check every quote, name and number before publishing. Researchers add a sixth: code the transcripts for themes.

This guide is part of our [interview transcription guide](/blog/interview-transcription), which covers formats, tools and consent. Here we pick up where the transcript ends.

## The 5-step transcript-to-draft workflow

| Step | What you do | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Organize | Name files consistently; build a source list | A folder you can search and trust |
| 2. Verify | Listen to every passage you might use; fix the transcript | Corrected passages with timestamps |
| 3. Find | Search each transcript for topics, claims and strong lines | A list of candidate quotes and facts |
| 4. Choose and attribute | Pick quotes, paraphrase the rest, apply AP rules | Draft paragraphs |
| 5. Check | Compare every quote to the audio; confirm names and facts | A draft you can defend |

## Step 1: organize many transcripts

One interview is easy. Twelve interviews over three weeks is where reporters lose quotes and researchers lose track of who said what.

**File names that sort and explain themselves:**

```
YYYY-MM-DD_Surname_format.ext

2026-11-04_Reyes_intelligent-verbatim.pdf
2026-11-04_Reyes_audio.m4a
2026-11-06_Okafor_intelligent-verbatim.pdf
P07_2026-10-21_intelligent-verbatim.pdf      (research: participant code, no name)
```

**A source list** (one row per interview) is the document you will open most:

| Source | Role | Date | Format | Ground rules | Contact | Key topics | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dana Reyes | Nurse, Brennan Clinic (fictional) | Nov. 4, 2026 | Intelligent verbatim | On the record | [phone] | Staffing, wait times | Quotes checked Nov. 7 |
| P07 | Participant, small-business owner | Oct. 21, 2026 | Intelligent verbatim | Anonymized | Via study coordinator | Payroll, cash flow | Codes 1st pass |

**Tags.** Add a short tag line under each transcript's header (`#staffing #wait-times #budget`) so a plain text search finds every interview on a topic.

## Step 2: verify the passages you might use

An automatic transcript is a draft. Before any line becomes a quote, listen to it.

- Play each candidate passage at normal speed, with the transcript in front of you.
- Fix words, speaker labels and punctuation that changes meaning.
- Note the timestamp next to every passage you correct.
- Mark anything you still cannot hear as `[inaudible hh:mm:ss]`. Do not quote around a gap.

Automatic tools can produce fluent sentences that are not in the audio; a 2024 study of Whisper found whole invented phrases in about 1% of the transcriptions tested. [1] Step 2 is how you catch them.

## Step 3: find the material (asking questions of transcripts with AI)

Reading every transcript again is the slow, reliable method. AI makes the search faster if you treat its answers as pointers, not as quotes.

**Good questions to ask a transcript:**

| Goal | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Find a topic | "Where does the speaker talk about staffing?" |
| Get dates and numbers | "What dates and numbers does she give for the doctor leaving?" |
| Find disagreement | "Does she contradict herself anywhere about when the waits started?" |
| Find feeling | "Where does she describe how patients reacted?" |
| Check a claim | "Does she say the clinic closed on Saturdays, or that it reduced hours?" |

**Rules for using the answers:**

1. Always open the passage in the transcript itself. The AI's wording is not the speaker's wording.
2. Go from the transcript to the timestamp, and from the timestamp to the audio.
3. Watch for merged speakers. An AI summary can attribute the interviewer's question to the interviewee.
4. Ask the same question of each transcript separately when you compare people, so you know which answer came from which interview.

## Step 4: choose quotes and paraphrase the rest

**What's quotable:**

| Quote it | Paraphrase it |
| --- | --- |
| Opinion and judgment: "We are voting on a map nobody has driven." | Facts and numbers: the district expects to save $1.4 million a year. |
| Emotion and experience: "Now she'll leave in the dark." | Long explanations and process |
| Vivid, original phrasing | Jargon the speaker used that readers won't know |
| Words that are themselves the news (an admission, a threat, a promise) | Anything you can say more clearly and accurately |

A useful test: take the quote marks off. If the sentence loses nothing, paraphrase it with attribution.

**Wrong and right, from one fictional transcript passage.** The speaker is invented:

```
[00:04:16] DANA REYES: I'd say it was probably around March, because
that's when Dr. Patel left, and we didn't have anyone to cover her
patients for three months.
```

| Version | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Wait times grew "around March, because that's when Dr. Patel left," Reyes said. | Acceptable partial quote, but the paraphrase below is cleaner |
| Wait times began growing around March, when a doctor left and the clinic had no one to cover her patients for three months, Reyes said. | Right: paraphrase keeps her uncertainty ("around") and attributes it |
| "The wait times started in March when Dr. Patel left," Reyes said. | Wrong: words inside quote marks she did not say, and "probably" is gone |
| Reyes said wait times started "in March." | Wrong: a partial quote that removes her hedge and changes the meaning |

## AP quote rules: attribution, "said," partial quotes and ellipses

| Rule | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use "said" | Reyes claimed the clinic was understaffed. | Reyes said the clinic was understaffed. |
| Name before verb | ..., said Reyes. | ..., Reyes said. |
| Verb first before a long title | ..., Dana Reyes, a nurse at the clinic for 11 years, said. | ..., said Dana Reyes, a nurse at the clinic for 11 years. |
| Full name and title first, last name after | Dana said... | First: Dana Reyes, a nurse at the clinic. Then: Reyes. |
| One attribution per quote | "We tried," she said. "We failed," she said. | "We tried. We failed," she said. |
| Punctuation inside the quote marks | "We had no one", she said. | "We had no one," she said. |
| Never change the words inside quote marks | Fixing grammar in a direct quote | Quote exactly, or paraphrase |
| Partial quotes sparingly | Stringing fragments: she was "tired," "angry" and "done" | Quote one full sentence, or paraphrase |
| Ellipses only where meaning survives | Cutting "not" or a qualifier | Cut only filler or asides; use a space on each side: ` ... ` |
| Brackets only for clarity | Adding words to sharpen a quote | Prefer paraphrase over `[the clinic]` insertions |

AP's Stylebook is updated every year; check the current quotations and attribution entries for edge cases. [2] For how quotes sit in a full story, see [how to write a news article](/blog/how-to-write-a-news-article), and for the wider rule set, the [AP style guide](/blog/ap-style-guide).

## For researchers: coding transcripts and finding themes

Qualitative researchers use the same transcripts differently: not for the best line, but for patterns across many interviews. The most cited method is thematic analysis, as set out by Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke in 2006. [3] It has six phases, and the authors stress that you move back and forth between them:

| Phase | What you do | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Familiarize yourself with the data | Read every transcript in full, ideally while listening; note first impressions | Memo of initial ideas |
| 2. Generate initial codes | Label meaningful segments with short codes | Coded transcripts |
| 3. Search for themes | Group codes into candidate themes | A theme map |
| 4. Review themes | Check themes against the coded extracts and the whole data set | Revised map |
| 5. Define and name themes | Write what each theme is, and is not | Theme definitions |
| 6. Produce the report | Write it up with vivid, representative extracts | The findings section |

**A codebook keeps coding consistent.** A fictional example:

```markdown
| Code | Definition | Include | Exclude | Example (participant, timestamp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| STAFF-GAP | Talk of unfilled roles or missing cover | Vacancies, leave, no locum | General workload complaints | P03, 00:04:16 |
| WORKAROUND | Informal fixes staff invent | Swapping shifts, triage notes | Official policy changes | P07, 00:12:02 |
| PATIENT-IMPACT | Effects on patients described by staff | Waits, missed care | Staff's own stress | P11, 00:22:40 |
```

**Transcription choices that matter for research:**

- Use the same format for every interview. Intelligent verbatim suits most thematic analysis; use true verbatim if hesitations, pauses or overlaps are part of what you study.
- Replace names with participant codes and remove identifying details as your ethics approval requires, before you share transcripts with co-coders.
- Keep a log of every change you make to a transcript.

AI can help you find candidate passages for a code ("where do participants mention covering shifts?"), but the coding decisions are yours, and they need to be traceable to the transcript.

## Accuracy and ethics: never change the meaning

The SPJ Code of Ethics asks journalists to verify information before releasing it, never deliberately distort facts or context, and take special care not to misrepresent or oversimplify. [4] For interview material, that means:

- **Context travels with the quote.** If the speaker was answering a hypothetical, say so.
- **Hedges stay.** "Probably," "I think" and "around" are part of what the person said.
- **Order matters.** Do not join two answers given ten minutes apart into one quote.
- **Check facts with the source,** such as dates, names, titles and numbers. Whether you read quotes back to a source, or let a source approve them, is a newsroom policy question, and policies differ. Know your outlet's rule before the interview.
- **Off the record stays off.** If the transcript marks a section off the record, it does not appear in the story or in shared files.
- **Research participants** are protected by the consent they signed. Do not use material outside what they agreed to.

**The final check before publishing:**

- [ ] Every direct quote matches the audio, word for word.
- [ ] Every paraphrase keeps the speaker's meaning and hedges.
- [ ] Every name, title and number is confirmed.
- [ ] Each quote is attributed to the right speaker.
- [ ] Nothing comes from an off-the-record section.

## FAQ

### What do you do after you transcribe an interview?

Follow five steps. Organize transcripts with consistent names and a source list. Verify the passages you might use against the audio. Find material by searching each transcript for topics and claims. Choose quotes and attribute them in AP style. Then check every quote, name and number with the recording and, where your newsroom allows, with the source.

### How do you choose which quotes to use?

Quote what only that person could say: opinion, emotion, first-hand experience and vivid phrasing. Paraphrase facts, numbers and long explanations, because you can state them more clearly yourself, with attribution. A good test: if the quote marks came off, would the sentence lose something? If not, paraphrase it.

### How do you attribute quotes in AP style?

Use said, in the past tense, and put the name before the verb: Reyes said. Identify the speaker by full name and title on first reference and by last name after that. Attribute once per quote, even if it runs several sentences, and put commas and periods inside the quotation marks.

### How do you transcribe interviews for thematic analysis?

Transcribe every interview in the same format, usually intelligent verbatim, or true verbatim if pauses and hesitations matter to your question. Label speakers, add timestamps, remove or replace identifying details as your ethics approval requires, and give each transcript a participant code. Then read every transcript in full before you start coding.

### Can AI find quotes in a transcript?

AI can point you to where a topic comes up, much faster than reading, but it can misquote, merge speakers or summarize something the person did not say. Use it to find passages, then read the passage in the transcript and listen to that spot in the audio before you quote it. Never paste an AI-supplied quote straight into a story.
