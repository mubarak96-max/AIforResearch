# The inverted pyramid in journalism: structure, examples and a template

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

The inverted pyramid is a news structure that puts the most important facts in the first paragraph and orders everything after it by falling importance. A reader who stops after one sentence still gets the news. An editor who needs to cut can cut from the bottom. It has three parts: the lead, the body and the tail.

This guide is part of our [AP style guide for news writers](/blog/ap-style-guide). Below: the structure, its disputed history, an annotated example story and a copyable template.

## The structure at a glance

```
 ______________________________________________
 \                                            /
  \   LEAD: who, what, when, where (why, how) /    1 sentence, 25 to 35 words
   \__________________________________________/
    \                                        /
     \  BODY: key details, evidence, quotes /      in order of importance
      \____________________________________/
       \                                  /
        \  TAIL: background, history,    /         first to be cut
         \  minor details               /
          \____________________________/
```

The test that defines the form is the cut test: delete paragraphs from the bottom, one at a time. After each cut, the story should still be complete and accurate, just shorter.

## Where the inverted pyramid came from (and what historians dispute)

Most textbooks tell a tidy story. The telegraph arrived in the 1840s. It was expensive, charged by length, and lines failed or were cut, especially during the Civil War. So reporters learned to send the most important facts first, in case the rest never arrived.

Parts of that story hold up. The telegraph made long dispatches costly and fed the rise of wire services such as the Associated Press, which sold the same copy to papers of every political stripe and so favored brief, nonpartisan reports. Poynter's Chip Scanlan quotes the communication scholar James Carey, who argued the telegraph provided "the underlying structure for one of the most influential literary styles of the 20th century." [1]

The Civil War part is weaker. Scanlan calls the idea that reporters invented the form because lines might be cut "a myth," because researchers found that most wartime stories were still told in time order. [1]

### The Stanton dispatches

Journalism historian David T. Z. Mindich makes the strongest case for a specific starting point. In *Just the Facts: How "Objectivity" Came to Define American Journalism* (NYU Press, 1998), he argues that the inverted pyramid was born with the coverage of Lincoln's death, and he credits Secretary of War Edwin Stanton. [2] Stanton controlled the military telegraph. His terse, impersonal dispatches about the assassination went to Gen. John A. Dix in New York and were printed by newspapers on April 15, 1865. The New York Herald's version included the line "The wound is mortal." [1] Compared with the chronological reporting of the day, a government bulletin that put the shooting and its outcome near the top was a sharp departure.

### The counterargument: it spread decades later

Content studies push the date forward. A team led by Marcus Errico at the University of Southern California sampled daily newspapers at five-year intervals from 1860 to 1910 and found the summary news lead was rare in the Civil War years: 2 of 2,043 stories in their 1860 sample, and none in their 1865 sample. Their conclusion is that the form did not come from wartime telegraph necessity. They link its spread to the reform era around the turn of the 20th century, when newspapers, journalism education and readers came to favor factual reporting over interpretive narrative. [3][4]

The fair summary: the telegraph, the wire services, Stanton's bulletins and later social changes all pushed in the same direction. No one story was the first, and the form took decades to become standard.

It lasted because it serves everyone downstream: the reader gets the news in seconds, the editor can fit a story to any space by cutting from the bottom, and a wire client can run 3 paragraphs or 15 of the same story.

## The lead: 5 Ws and 1 H, with 3 examples

The lead is the first sentence of a news story. It answers the most important of the 5 Ws and 1 H: who, what, when, where, why and how. You rarely fit all six. Pick the two or three that make this story news and push the rest into paragraph two.

Ask one question before you write: what happened that did not happen yesterday?

The three leads below are fictional examples. The people, places and numbers are invented.

**1. Hard news lead (what and who lead)**

> The Brennan County school board voted 5-2 Tuesday to close two elementary schools next June, moving about 600 students to three other buildings.

What, who, when and the effect. Why goes in paragraph two.

**2. Impact lead (the reader comes first)**

> Drivers who use the Route 9 bridge will face a 14-mile detour starting Monday, when state crews close the span for six months of repairs.

The news is the effect on readers, so it leads. The agency and the reason follow.

**3. Delayed identification lead (who is not yet famous)**

> A retired nurse who spent 30 years at the county hospital has been charged with stealing more than $40,000 from a patient charity she ran, police said Thursday.

When the person is not well known, describe them in the lead and name them in paragraph two. Reporters call this a delayed identification lead, and use it for people readers would not recognize by name.

### Lead mistakes to fix

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| "On Tuesday night, at a meeting held at the district office, the board..." (starts with time and place) | "The Brennan County school board voted Tuesday..." |
| "The school board discussed several issues Tuesday." (no news) | Lead with the decision. |
| A 60-word lead with four clauses | Keep it near 25 to 35 words; move details down. |

## Ordering the body by importance

After the lead, each paragraph answers the next question a reader would ask. A reliable order for a hard news story:

1. **Paragraph 2: the why or how**, and the name if the lead delayed it.
2. **Paragraph 3: the best quote**, from the person with the most at stake or the most authority.
3. **Paragraphs 4 to 6: supporting facts**: numbers, the document or study, the vote count, the cost.
4. **Paragraphs 7 and 8: the other side**: reaction, dissent, the response from the person accused.
5. **Paragraphs 9 and 10: what happens next**: dates, deadlines, the next meeting.
6. **The tail: background**: history, earlier votes, related events.

Keep one idea per paragraph, so paragraphs are easy to cut and reorder. And remember that fairness is not tail material: a response from someone accused of wrongdoing belongs high in the story, not in the last line where it will be cut.

## Full annotated example

The story below is a fictional example. Brennan County, its school board and every person and number in it are invented.

```
BRENNAN FALLS, Ore. (fictional) — The Brennan County school board voted 5-2
Tuesday to close two elementary schools next June, moving about 600 students
to three other buildings.                                      [1 LEAD]

The board said falling enrollment and a $3.1 million budget gap left it no
choice. District enrollment has dropped 18% since 2016, according to a
district report presented at the meeting.                      [2 WHY + EVIDENCE]

"No one on this board wanted to do this," board chair Maria Okafor said.
"But we can't keep paying to heat half-empty buildings while we cut
teachers."                                                     [3 BEST QUOTE]

Lincoln and Westview elementary schools will close. Their students will move
to Ridge, Oakmont and Fairview elementary schools, which the district says
have room for about 750 more students.                         [4 DETAILS]

The district expects to save about $1.4 million a year, mostly in building
and staff costs, the report says.                              [5 NUMBERS]

About 150 parents attended, and 22 spoke against the plan. Several said the
change would add 20 minutes or more to their children's bus rides.
                                                               [6 REACTION]

Board member Dan Reyes, who voted no, said the district had not studied bus
routes. "We are voting on a map nobody has driven," he said.   [7 DISSENT]

The district will hold meetings for affected families in January and
publish new bus routes by March 1.                             [8 NEXT STEPS]

Lincoln Elementary opened in 1954 and was renovated in 1998. Westview opened
in 1971.                                                       [9 TAIL]

The board last closed a school in 2009, when it merged two middle
schools.                                                       [10 TAIL]
```

**What the notes show.** Cut after paragraph 1 and a reader knows the decision. Cut after paragraph 3 and they know why, from the decision-maker. Cut after paragraph 8 and the story is still complete and fair, with dissent and next steps. Paragraphs 9 and 10 are the tail: delete them and nothing breaks. Every number names its source, and attribution uses "said" (see [how to write a news article](/blog/how-to-write-a-news-article) for datelines and attribution).

## Inverted pyramid template (copy and paste)

Copy this into any editor and replace the brackets. Delete the hints in parentheses before filing.

```markdown
# [Headline: subject + active verb + key fact, under 70 characters]

[CITY IN CAPS, State abbreviation] — [LEAD: who did what, when, where, with the
one fact that makes it news. 25 to 35 words.]

[WHY OR HOW: the reason or cause. Name the person here if the lead used delayed
identification. Name the source of any number.]

"[BEST QUOTE: one or two sentences that add feeling or authority, not facts you
could paraphrase]," [full name], [title], said.

[SUPPORTING FACT 1: the most important detail, number or document.]

[SUPPORTING FACT 2.]

[REACTION OR OTHER SIDE: the response from anyone criticized or affected.]

[WHAT HAPPENS NEXT: dates, deadlines, next decision.]

[TAIL: background and history. Safe to cut.]

[TAIL: minor detail. Safe to cut.]

---
Sources checked: [document 1, link] · [interview 1, date] · [interview 2, date]
```

**Inverted pyramid news release.** The same template works for a press release: replace the byline with your organization, add a contact block and boilerplate, and end with `###`. See [how to write a press release](/blog/how-to-write-a-press-release) for the full format.

## When not to use the inverted pyramid

The pyramid is built for speed. Some stories need something else.

| Structure | How it works | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inverted pyramid | Most important facts first, falling importance | Breaking news, briefs, meetings, crime, business results, press releases |
| Delayed lead with nut graf | Opens with a scene, person or anecdote; a "nut graf" in paragraph 3 to 5 says why the story matters now | Features, trend stories, explainers |
| Hourglass | A short summary top, a "turn" that signals a narrative, then the events in time order | Dramatic events with a clear sequence: rescues, trials, disasters |
| Chronological narrative | Beginning, middle, end | Long-form, profiles, reconstructions |

The **nut graf** is the paragraph in a feature that tells the reader what the story is about and why it matters now.

The **hourglass** was named by Poynter's Roy Peter Clark in 1983. Its top delivers the most significant information, so a reader who stops there is still informed; the turn, often an attribution such as "police gave this account," starts the narrative. [5]

## Why the inverted pyramid suits SEO and AI answers

Answer-first writing is the inverted pyramid applied to the web:

1. **Snippets.** Google's featured snippets show a passage from the page above the link, chosen from the page content itself. [6] A self-contained answer in the first paragraph is easy to lift.
2. **AI answers.** Assistants that quote pages need a direct, factual passage that answers the question. A one-sentence lead is exactly that.
3. **Readers scan.** On a phone, a reader sees the headline and first paragraph before deciding to scroll.

For blog posts, reports and explainers, use a 40 to 60 word direct answer under the headline, then detail, then background. This page does. Google's guidance still asks for people-first content with original value, not pages written to game rankings, so answer first and then earn the reader's time. [7]

## FAQ

### What is the inverted pyramid in journalism?

The inverted pyramid is a news structure that puts the most important facts in the first paragraph and orders everything after it by falling importance. The lead answers who, what, when and where; the body adds detail, quotes and context; the tail holds background a reader or editor can cut without losing the story.

### Who invented the inverted pyramid?

No single person invented it. The popular story credits Civil War telegraph reporters, and historian David Mindich points to the War Department's dispatches on Lincoln's death in April 1865. Content studies of 19th-century newspapers found the summary lead was still rare in 1865 and spread over the following decades, so many historians now describe a gradual change.

### What are the three parts of the inverted pyramid?

The three parts are the lead, the body and the tail. The lead is one sentence or paragraph with the core news. The body gives supporting facts, quotes and evidence in order of importance. The tail holds background, history and minor detail, the material an editor can trim first if the story runs long.

### When should you not use the inverted pyramid?

Skip it for features, profiles, narrative stories and essays, where suspense, scene or character matters more than speed. Those stories often use a delayed lead with a nut graf, or the hourglass, which opens with a short summary and then tells the rest in time order. Breaking news, briefs and press releases still suit the pyramid.

### Do press releases use the inverted pyramid?

Yes. A press release written in news style uses the inverted pyramid: a headline, a dateline, a lead with the news in one sentence, supporting facts and a quote, then the boilerplate about the organization. Journalists skim releases from the top, so anything important that sits in paragraph five may never be read.
