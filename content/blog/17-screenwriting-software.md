# Screenwriting software in 2026: the best options for every budget

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

The best screenwriting software for most writers in 2026 is Fade In if you want to pay once (about $80), WriterDuet or Arc Studio if you co-write, Highland Pro if you live on a Mac and iPad, and Final Draft if your producers expect it. Trelby and Beat are the best free picks. All of them format; none of them does your research.

Screenwriting software is a program that formats a script into standard screenplay layout as you type: scene headings, action, character cues, dialogue and transitions, each at the right indent, with page breaks handled for you. That one job is what you are paying for. This guide compares the main options on price, platforms, collaboration and Final Draft (`.fdx`) compatibility, then covers the part of the job no formatter handles: the research, treatments, series bibles and notes that sit around the script.

Prices below were checked on each vendor's site in November 2026. They change often, and holiday sales are common, so confirm before you buy.

## Short answer: the best pick by budget and platform

| If you are... | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| On a budget of $0 | **Trelby** (Windows, Linux) or **Beat** (macOS) | Free, open source, real formatting, `.fdx` export |
| Willing to pay once, never again | **Fade In** | About $80, free updates, Windows, Mac and Linux, opens and saves `.fdx` |
| Co-writing with a partner | **WriterDuet** or **Arc Studio** | Real-time co-writing in the browser and desktop apps |
| Writing on Mac, iPad and iPhone | **Highland Pro** | Native Apple apps, iCloud sync, Fountain plain text |
| Delivering to a studio or staffed show | **Final Draft 13** | The long-standing industry file format; real-time collaboration between FD13 users |
| Writing and also prepping a shoot | **StudioBinder** or **Celtx** | Script editor tied to breakdowns, schedules and call sheets |
| On Chromebook or a shared computer | **WriterDuet**, **Arc Studio** or **Celtx** in the browser | Nothing to install |

If you only remember one thing: whatever you choose, make sure it can export to `.fdx` and PDF. A PDF is what readers, contests and agents read. An `.fdx` is what a co-writer, producer or production office may ask for.

### What each one is best at

**Final Draft 13.** Final Draft is the name most US studios and showrunners know, and its `.fdx` file is what many production tools expect. Version 13 lets multiple writers edit a script and its Beat Board at the same time, but only with other Final Draft 13 users. You can buy it outright (one license runs on two computers) or subscribe to the Suite, which adds Final Draft Cloud. The cost is the main reason writers look elsewhere; see [Final Draft alternatives compared](/blog/final-draft-alternatives).

**Fade In.** The strongest value in paid screenwriting software. You pay once, updates to the current version are free, and one license covers your own computers on Windows, Mac and Linux. It opens and saves `.fdx` files, so you can work with a Final Draft shop without owning Final Draft. Collaboration, cloud storage and phone access come through Fade In Access, an optional monthly or annual subscription.

**WriterDuet.** Built for co-writing: two writers in the same scene, cursor by cursor, in a browser. The free plan gives you three projects. Paid plans add unlimited projects, desktop apps and offline writing that syncs later. WriterSolo is a free offline version without the collaboration features.

**Highland Pro.** A Mac, iPad and iPhone app from Quote-Unquote Apps that writes in Fountain, a plain-text screenplay markup. You type `INT. KITCHEN - NIGHT` and it becomes a scene heading. Highland Pro replaced Highland 2 and is sold as one subscription across Apple devices. It imports and exports Final Draft files.

**Arc Studio.** A modern editor with outlining, a "Stash" for cut material, and season outlines on the Pro plan. Every plan, including free, has real-time co-writing and Final Draft import and export, but the free plan is capped at two scripts and watermarks PDFs.

**Celtx.** Once a free desktop app, now a browser-based studio with screenplay, story development and production planning tools. Good for film students and small crews who want the script connected to breakdowns. The free tier allows one project.

**StudioBinder.** A production management platform (call sheets, shot lists, schedules) with a free screenplay editor attached. Pick it if you are also producing what you write.

**Trelby.** A free, open-source formatter for Windows and Linux. It looks dated, but it formats correctly, works offline and exports PDF, Fountain and `.fdx`.

**Beat.** A free, open-source macOS app that saves in Fountain and imports and exports `.fdx` with notes and revisions. Its developer says the desktop app will stay free and that there will be no Windows or Linux version. Files stay on your device.

## Best free screenwriting software

The best free screenwriting software depends on your operating system:

| Your computer | Best free pick | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows | Trelby, or WriterSolo | No real-time collaboration; Trelby's interface is old |
| Mac | Beat | No collaboration; macOS and iOS only |
| Linux | Trelby | No collaboration |
| Chromebook or any browser | WriterDuet free plan | 3 projects |
| Any device, with a co-writer | Arc Studio free plan | 2 scripts, watermarked PDF |

Two warnings about "free":

1. **Free tiers often cap projects, not pages.** WriterDuet allows three projects, Arc Studio two, Celtx one. That is enough for a first feature but not for a writer with a drawer of drafts and episodes.
2. **Check the export before you commit.** A free tool that watermarks PDFs or locks `.fdx` export behind a paywall is a trial, not a free tool. Trelby and Beat have neither restriction.

Google Docs and Word also work for short scripts with add-ons or styles; the [screenplay format guide](/blog/screenplay-format) shows how, with exact margins.

## Best screenwriting software for collaboration and writers' rooms

Collaborative screenwriting software lets two or more writers edit the same script, either live or by passing a file with tracked revisions. Here is how each option handles it:

| Software | How co-writing works | What every collaborator needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WriterDuet | Live, in the browser or app; revision history | A WriterDuet account (paid for full features) |
| Arc Studio | Live co-writing and comments on all plans | Inviting collaborators requires a paid plan |
| Final Draft 13 | Live co-editing of the script and Beat Board | Final Draft 13 (FD13 only collaborates with FD13) |
| Fade In | Through Fade In Access: share, co-edit, publish | A Fade In Access subscription |
| Celtx | Share a project with co-writers | Paid seats on Writer and Writer Pro plans |
| Highland Pro, Beat, Trelby | Trade files; Highland has comments for partner notes | The same app, or any app that reads Fountain or `.fdx` |

For a two-person team, WriterDuet and Arc Studio are the easiest: send a link and start writing. For a staffed TV room, the script is only one of the documents the room touches each day. The others, the series bible, the season arc, the research, the network notes, live somewhere else, and that somewhere is usually a pile of shared Google Docs.

## What screenwriting software doesn't do

Every tool above is very good at one thing: turning your words into a correctly formatted script. Very little of the work of getting a script made happens inside that format. Before and around the screenplay you write:

| Document | What it is | Where most writers keep it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Treatment** | A prose telling of the story in present tense, from a few pages to 20 or more | Word or Google Docs |
| **Series bible** | The show's world, characters, tone, rules and season arcs; the reference every writer in the room works from | Google Docs, Notion |
| **Outline or beat sheet** | Scene by scene, before you draft | Index cards in the formatter, or a separate doc |
| **Research file** | Facts, sources, period details, expert interviews | Bookmarks, PDFs, notebooks |
| **Coverage and notes** | Reader reports and producer notes on a draft | Email, PDF, Word |
| **Pitch document or deck** | The one-pager or deck you take into a meeting | Word, Slides |

Some formatters have outlining tools (index cards, beat boards, Arc Studio's season outlines), and those help. But none of them is built to hold a 40-page bible with linked sources, a folder of court records, or a reader's coverage that three people are commenting on. Script coverage is a written report in which a reader summarizes a screenplay and judges whether it is worth a producer's time, ending in a Pass, Consider or Recommend. If you have never written or read one, see [script coverage examples and a free template](/blog/script-coverage).

Research is the biggest gap. Scripts based on real events, set in a profession (medicine, law, science) or in another period need sources you can find again and defend. A formatter has nowhere to put them. The [guide to researching a screenplay](/blog/how-to-research-a-screenplay) covers the method in full.

## A full kit: formatter plus research and development workspace

A practical setup for most writers has two parts:

1. **A formatter** from the table above, for the script itself. Export PDF for readers and `.fdx` for collaborators.
2. **A development workspace** for everything around the script: treatment, bible, outline, research with sources, and notes.

You can build the second part from Google Docs and a folder of PDFs. It works until you need to find which document said the hospital's night shift starts at 7, or which article your consultant sent, or what changed in the bible between the pilot and episode four.

### Where WriteGrid fits (and what it does not do)

WriteGrid is a web editor for research-backed writing. To be plain about it: **WriteGrid does not format screenplays and does not open `.fdx` files.** Keep writing the script in Final Draft, Fade In, WriterDuet or any formatter above. Use WriteGrid for the development documents and research around it:

- **Treatments and series bibles in a shared document.** Real-time co-editing, comments, suggestions (tracked changes) and version history, so the room sees who changed a character's backstory and when. WriteGrid opens `.docx` files and keeps their styles, so an existing Word bible comes in intact, and you can export back to Word or PDF.
- **Research with sources.** Search 250M+ scholarly works from inside the document and type `@` to cite one. Useful when a medical drama needs the real literature on sepsis, or a period piece needs the social history behind a scene.
- **Ask one PDF at a time.** Upload archive scans, court filings, reports or your own script PDF to the Library, open one, and use Ask to question it. Answers carry page numbers, so you can check them: "Which page of the inquest report describes the first call to the fire brigade?" or, on your script, "Where does the midpoint land?"
- **AI review as comments.** Run claim checks on a research bible or a pitch document before it goes to a producer. Results appear as comments in the text, next to the line they concern.
- **AI Chat beside the document** for brainstorming loglines or tightening a treatment. It can draw on the web, your Library and the document itself as sources, and cites the real papers it uses. Your own judgment stays the final call.

## How to choose: a five-question checklist

- [ ] **What do you deliver?** If a studio or showrunner requires Final Draft, buy Final Draft or make sure your tool exports clean `.fdx`.
- [ ] **Do you co-write live?** WriterDuet or Arc Studio. Otherwise collaboration features are not worth paying for.
- [ ] **One-time or subscription?** Fade In (once, about $80) or Final Draft perpetual; or free with Trelby, Beat or WriterSolo.
- [ ] **Which devices?** Apple-only writers get the most from Highland Pro or Beat. Linux users: Fade In or Trelby. Chromebook: browser tools.
- [ ] **Where will the research, bible and notes live?** Decide now, before they spread across email threads.

Download the trial or free version of your top two and write the same five pages in each. Formatting is standard; how a tool feels when you type is not.

## FAQ

### What software do professional screenwriters use?

Final Draft has been the long-standing standard in US film and TV, and its .fdx file is the format producers, studios and production software most often expect. Many working writers also use Fade In, WriterDuet, Highland or Arc Studio and export to .fdx or PDF when they deliver. What matters professionally is clean standard formatting and a file others can open, not the brand of the formatter.

### Is there free screenwriting software?

Yes. Trelby (Windows and Linux) and Beat (macOS) are free, open-source formatters, and both handle Final Draft files: Trelby can export .fdx and Beat imports and exports it. WriterDuet has a free plan limited to three projects plus a free offline app, WriterSolo. Arc Studio's free plan allows two scripts with a watermark on PDF exports. Celtx and StudioBinder also have free tiers with tight limits.

### What is the best screenwriting software for collaboration?

WriterDuet and Arc Studio were built around real-time co-writing in the browser, so co-writers can type in the same script at once on almost any device. Final Draft 13 added real-time collaboration, but every collaborator needs Final Draft 13. Fade In collaborates through its optional Fade In Access subscription. For a writers' room, also plan where the bible, research and notes will live.

### Is there screenwriting software with a one-time purchase instead of a subscription?

Yes. Final Draft 13 sells a perpetual license (list price about $249.99, often discounted) alongside its subscription suite, and Fade In costs about $80 once with free updates to the current version. Trelby, Beat and WriterSolo are free. Highland Pro, Arc Studio, WriterDuet's paid plans and Celtx are subscriptions.

### Can I write a screenplay in Google Docs or Word?

You can, with paragraph styles in Word or an add-on such as Screenplay Docs or Fountainize in Google Docs. It works for a short film or a first draft, but you have to manage page breaks, (MORE) and (CONT'D) yourself or trust the add-on. For a feature you plan to send out, a dedicated formatter saves time and produces a cleaner PDF.
