# Primary vs secondary sources: what the difference is and how to tell, in any field

*Last updated: 14 November 2026*

A primary source is first-hand evidence: a document, object, dataset or study created by people directly involved, at the time. A secondary source analyzes or interprets primary sources from a distance, such as a scholarly book or a review article. A tertiary source, such as an encyclopedia or textbook, summarizes both. Whether a source is primary depends on your research question, not only on the source itself.

This guide shows how the three categories work in history, the sciences, law and business, gives a decision table you can apply to any source, and covers the cases that confuse people most. To judge whether a source is trustworthy, a separate question, see [how to evaluate source credibility](/blog/how-to-evaluate-source-credibility).

## What is a primary source?

**A primary source is the raw material of research: evidence created by a participant or witness, or the original report of new data.** The Library of Congress describes primary sources as the raw materials of history: original documents and objects created at the time under study [1].

Common primary sources:

- Letters, diaries, speeches, interviews and memoirs
- Government records, laws, treaties and court judgments
- Photographs, recordings, maps and artifacts
- Original research articles reporting new experiments, surveys or analyses
- Datasets, lab notebooks and field notes
- Company filings, annual reports and patents
- Novels, poems, films and artworks, when you study them as works

## What is a secondary source?

**A secondary source is one step removed: someone who was not part of the event or study explains, interprets or evaluates primary sources.** It brings an argument, a summary or a comparison.

Common secondary sources:

- Scholarly books and monographs that interpret evidence
- Review articles, systematic reviews and meta-analyses
- Biographies and histories written later
- Law review articles, treatises and legal commentary
- Literary criticism and film criticism
- Industry analyses and news features written after the event

## What is a tertiary source?

**A tertiary source condenses primary and secondary sources into reference material for quick lookup.** Encyclopedias, textbooks, dictionaries, handbooks, almanacs, bibliographies and databases of abstracts are tertiary.

Use tertiary sources at the start of a project: to learn the vocabulary, find the main debates and get the names of key authors and studies. Then follow their references. Our guide to [finding sources for a research paper](/blog/how-to-find-sources-for-a-research-paper) covers where to go next.

### Primary vs secondary vs tertiary at a glance

| | Primary | Secondary | Tertiary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distance from the event or data | Direct | One step removed | Two or more steps removed |
| What it gives you | Evidence | Interpretation and argument | Summary and background |
| Who made it | A participant, witness or the original researchers | A scholar or writer working from primary sources | A compiler or editor working from many sources |
| Typical use in a paper | The evidence you analyze | The debate you join and the context | Background you usually do not cite |
| Example | Watson and Crick's 1953 paper on the structure of DNA [5] | A review of the history of DNA research | An encyclopedia entry on DNA |

## Primary and secondary sources by discipline

**Each field draws the line in a slightly different place, so learn your field's usage before you count sources for an assignment.** The four tables below use real, checkable sources.

### History

History uses the terms most strictly. A primary source comes from the period you study; a secondary source is a later historian's account. Historians read primary sources by asking who made them, when, why and for whom, a habit Sam Wineburg calls "sourcing" [3].

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | The Declaration of Independence (1776); letters between John and Abigail Adams; a 1918 newspaper report on influenza |
| Secondary | Bernard Bailyn, *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution* (Harvard University Press, 1967) |
| Tertiary | An encyclopedia entry on the American Revolution; a survey textbook |

### Sciences and health

In the sciences, the *primary literature* is the original research article that reports new data. Review articles are secondary literature, and textbooks are tertiary.

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | Jinek et al. (2012), the *Science* paper that showed CRISPR-Cas9 can be programmed to cut chosen DNA sequences [6] |
| Secondary | Doudna and Charpentier (2014), a review in *Science* of genome engineering with CRISPR-Cas9 [7] |
| Tertiary | A molecular biology textbook chapter on gene editing |

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are secondary sources in this scheme, even though they are high in the evidence hierarchy for many questions. "Secondary" says how far a source is from the data, not how good it is. Our [levels of evidence guide](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) covers quality, and [what is a systematic review](/blog/what-is-a-systematic-review) covers how reviews are built.

### Law

Lawyers speak of primary and secondary *authority*. Primary authority is the law itself; secondary authority explains it and can persuade a court, but does not bind it [4].

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); the US Constitution; a statute; an agency regulation |
| Secondary | A law review article; a legal treatise; the Restatements of the law published by the American Law Institute |
| Tertiary | A legal encyclopedia or a legal dictionary entry |

Restatements are a common trap. Courts cite them often, but they are secondary authority unless a court adopts a section, in which case the court's judgment is the primary authority. For citation format, see the [Bluebook generator](/tools/citation-generator/bluebook).

### Business and economics

Business research mixes historical, legal and statistical sources. Company documents and official statistics are primary; analyses of them are secondary.

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | A company's Form 10-K annual report filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, searchable in EDGAR [8]; official statistics; interview transcripts you collected |
| Secondary | An analyst report on the company; a peer-reviewed case study; a business press feature |
| Tertiary | A business encyclopedia or a textbook chapter on corporate strategy |

Researchers in business and social science also distinguish *primary data* (data you collect yourself through surveys, interviews or experiments) from *secondary data* (data someone else collected, such as a national census or a published dataset). The terms overlap with primary and secondary sources but are not the same. A census table is secondary data for you, yet it is still a primary source in the document sense. Public datasets such as [FRED economic data](/blog/fred-economic-data-for-research) are a common source of secondary data.

## How do you tell if a source is primary or secondary?

**Ask what your research question is about, then ask whether the source is direct evidence of that thing or someone's account of it.** The same document can change category when the question changes.

### Decision table

Work down the table and stop at the first row that matches.

| Question to ask | If yes | Example (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the source itself the thing you are studying? | Primary | Studying how newspapers covered vaccines: every article is primary, even opinion pieces |
| Was it created by a participant or eyewitness, at or near the time? | Primary | A nurse's diary from a 1918 influenza ward |
| Does it report data or findings the authors produced themselves? | Primary | A trial report, a survey study, a lab experiment, a company's own filing |
| Is it the text of a law, judgment, treaty or official record? | Primary | A statute, a court opinion, parliamentary minutes |
| Does it analyze, interpret or compare other sources, written later or by someone not involved? | Secondary | A review article, a biography, a law review article, a history book |
| Does it summarize established knowledge for reference, with no new argument? | Tertiary | An encyclopedia, a textbook, a dictionary |

### Context changes the answer

Librarians make the same point. The ACRL *Framework for Information Literacy* asks students to judge a source by how and why it was created and by the context in which it is used, not by its format alone [2].

| Source | Primary when your question is about… | Secondary when your question is about… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A 1960s biology textbook | How biology was taught in the 1960s | Cell biology (it would be tertiary, and out of date) |
| A historian's 1950 book on the Civil War | How historians wrote about the war in the 1950s | The Civil War itself |
| A review article on remote work | The way researchers framed remote work during 2020 | Whether remote work affects productivity |
| A news interview with a CEO | The CEO's public statements | The company's strategy, if the article adds analysis |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "primary" as "better".** Primary sources need interpretation and can be biased, partial or wrong. A diary records one person's view. A single study can fail to replicate.
- **Calling every journal article primary.** Review articles, commentaries and editorials are secondary. Check the article type and the methods section.
- **Citing the summary instead of the original.** If a review reports a statistic, find and cite the original study, and check it says what the review claims. Our guide on [how to find references for a claim](/blog/how-to-find-references-for-a-claim) shows how.
- **Citing tertiary sources as evidence.** Encyclopedias and textbooks are for background. Most instructors will not accept them as evidence.
- **Using a translation or transcription without saying so.** An edited or translated edition of a primary source adds an editor's choices. Cite the edition you used.

### Checklist for each source

- [ ] What is my research question about?
- [ ] Is this source direct evidence of that, or an account of it?
- [ ] Who created it, when, and why?
- [ ] If it is a journal article, is it original research or a review?
- [ ] If it is secondary, have I found and read the primary sources it relies on for the points I use?
- [ ] Have I recorded the type in my notes, so I can meet the assignment's requirements?

## FAQ

### What is the difference between primary and secondary sources?

A primary source is first-hand evidence created at the time or by the people involved: a letter, a dataset, a court judgment, an original research article. A secondary source analyzes, interprets or summarizes primary sources from a distance: a history book, a review article, a law review article. The same document can be either, depending on what your research question is about.

### Is a journal article a primary or secondary source?

It depends on the article. A research article that reports a new experiment, survey or analysis is a primary source in the sciences and social sciences. A review article, a systematic review or a commentary that discusses other studies is a secondary source. Read the abstract and methods: if the authors collected or analyzed the data themselves, treat it as primary.

### What is a tertiary source?

A tertiary source collects and condenses primary and secondary sources into a quick reference: encyclopedias, textbooks, dictionaries, handbooks, fact books and indexes. Tertiary sources are useful for background, definitions and finding key terms and authors. Most instructors do not accept them as evidence in a research paper, so follow them to the secondary and primary sources they rely on.

### Is a newspaper article a primary or secondary source?

A news report written at the time of an event is usually a primary source for that event, especially an eyewitness account or interview. A later news feature that analyzes events from a distance is secondary. For a study of how the media covered a topic, any article is primary, because the coverage itself is what you are studying.

### Are textbooks primary or secondary sources?

Textbooks are usually tertiary sources, because they summarize a field's established knowledge from many secondary sources. Some people call them secondary. Either way, cite the original research rather than a textbook in a research paper. The exception is research about education or the history of a subject, where an old textbook can be a primary source.

### Do I need primary sources for a research paper?

Usually yes. Most research papers need primary sources as evidence and secondary sources to show what others have argued. History and law assignments often require a minimum number of primary sources. In the sciences, the original research articles are your primary literature. Check your assignment brief, and ask your instructor if it does not say.
