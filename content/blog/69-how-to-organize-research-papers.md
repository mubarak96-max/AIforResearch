# How to organize research papers so you can find them again

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

To organize research papers, keep every paper in one reference manager with its PDF attached, file papers in a few broad collections by project, use tags for themes and reading status, name PDFs as author, year and title, write a short note on each paper you read, merge duplicates, and spend 30 minutes a week clearing your inbox.

This guide is for students and researchers whose papers are scattered across downloads folders, browser tabs and email. It is part of our series on finding and checking sources; the pillar guide on [levels of evidence](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) covers which papers deserve your reading time first.

## How do researchers organize papers?

Researchers who can find any paper in seconds usually follow one rule: every paper lives in one place, as a record with its PDF attached. The rest is detail.

**A reference manager is software that stores bibliographic records (author, year, title, journal, DOI) with their PDFs and notes, and formats citations in your writing.** Zotero, Mendeley and EndNote are common examples; our [Zotero vs. Mendeley](/blog/zotero-vs-mendeley) comparison covers the choice. Which one you pick matters less than using one consistently.

The workflow has five stages:

| Stage | What happens | Where it lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capture | Save the record and PDF the moment you find a paper | Reference manager inbox (an "Unfiled" or "Inbox" collection) |
| File | Put it in a project collection and tag it | Collections and tags |
| Read | Read, annotate, set its status | PDF reader linked to the record |
| Note | Write a short structured note | A note attached to the record |
| Cite | Insert citations while writing | Word processor plugin or editor |

Most disorder comes from skipping capture ("I'll save it later") or filing (an inbox of 400 papers). The weekly routine below fixes both.

## Should you organize research papers with folders or tags?

Use folders for where a paper belongs and tags for what it is about. You need both, and a good reference manager lets one paper sit in several places.

**A collection is a reference manager's folder: a named group of records, often nested.** In Zotero, collections work like playlists, not like folders on your disk: "Adding an item to multiple collections does not duplicate the item." [1] Deleting a collection leaves its papers in the library.

**A tag is a keyword you attach to a record, so you can pull together every paper that shares a theme, method or status across collections.** Zotero lets you assign colors and number keys to up to 9 tags, so you can tag a selected paper with one keystroke. [1]

| | Collections (folders) | Tags |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best for | Projects, chapters, a review, a course | Themes, methods, populations, reading status |
| How many | 5 to 15 at the top level | As many as you need, but reuse the same words |
| One paper in several? | Yes, in Zotero, without copies [1] | Yes |
| Travels when you share or copy items | Not in Zotero [1] | Yes, in Zotero [1] |
| Typical mistake | Nesting 5 levels deep | Synonyms: "RCT", "rct", "randomised trial" |

**Decision rule:** if you would put the paper in your bibliography for one piece of work, that work is a collection. If you would search for it by topic or method, that is a tag.

A tag vocabulary that works for most theses:

- **Status:** `0-to-read`, `1-skimmed`, `2-read`, `3-cited` (numbers keep them sorted)
- **Design:** `rct`, `cohort`, `qualitative`, `review` (see [study types](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types))
- **Theme:** two to five words from your research question
- **Role:** `key-paper`, `method-source`, `counterevidence`

Write the vocabulary down and keep it short. Ten well-used tags beat eighty used once.

### Use saved searches as smart folders

**A saved search is a stored query that updates itself as records change, so it behaves like a folder that fills and empties automatically.** In Zotero a saved search for "Tag is not `2-read`" gives you a live reading list. [1] Make three: to read, read but no note, and key papers.

## How should you name research paper PDFs?

Name every PDF as first author, year and short title: `Smith - 2021 - Exercise and depression in older adults.pdf`. Files then sort by author, and you can recognize a file outside your reference manager.

Zotero 7 does this automatically. Its default template is `{{ firstCreator suffix=" - " }}{{ year suffix=" - " }}{{ title truncate="100" }}`, which produces names like `Lee et al. - 2023 - Title.pdf`, and you can edit the template in the General settings. [2] If you name files by hand:

1. Use the first author's family name only.
2. Use the four-digit year. Add `a`, `b` if one author has two papers that year.
3. Shorten the title to six to ten words and drop leading articles.
4. Keep the separator the same in every file.
5. Avoid characters some systems reject: `/ \ : * ? " < > |`.

Never leave files named `download.pdf`, `1-s2.0-S0140673621.pdf` or `fulltext(3).pdf`. You won't find them by search, and you will download them twice.

## What is the best way to store PDFs for research?

Store each PDF attached to its record in your reference manager, synced to a second place and backed up. Three rules prevent most losses:

1. **One copy only.** Two copies means annotations split between them.
2. **Attach, don't link to a downloads folder.** Linked files break when you move or clean up folders.
3. **Back up the library itself**, not only the PDFs. Your collections, tags and notes live in the library's database, not in the files.

Check your storage limit before a large review. A thesis literature review can easily pass several hundred PDFs.

## How do you keep notes that travel with the paper?

Attach a note to the paper's record, not in a separate notebook. A note in the record moves with the paper into every collection, and it's there when you cite.

Use the same five lines for every paper you read:

```
Question: what the study asked, in one line
Method: design, sample size, setting, years
Finding: the main result, with the number and page (p. 7)
Limits: what the authors admit, and what you noticed
Use: where it goes in your work (Ch. 2, section on adherence)
```

The page number in "Finding" saves you an hour when you write. The "Use" line stops you from rereading papers to remember why you saved them.

## How do you remove duplicate papers?

Merge duplicates rather than deleting one copy, so notes, tags and collections are kept. Duplicates creep in when you import from several databases or save the same paper twice.

Zotero finds possible duplicates in its Duplicate Items view. It compares titles, DOIs and ISBNs, and also checks that years are within one year and at least one author's last name and first initial match. [3] Merging keeps all collections and tags from the merged items, and citations already in your documents keep working. [3]

Run a duplicate check after every large import and before you export references for a review.

## How do you keep track of research papers for a thesis or review?

Keep a reading status on every paper and a short search log for every search you run. Together they answer "have I seen this?" and "where did this come from?".

A search log needs five columns: date, database, the exact search string, filters, and the number of results. Our guide on [how to search PubMed](/blog/how-to-search-pubmed) shows what a search string looks like, and [citation snowballing](/blog/citation-snowballing) explains how to log papers found through references.

## A 30-minute weekly routine

Once a week, at the same time, do this:

1. **Empty the inbox.** File every new item into a collection (5 minutes).
2. **Fix records.** Check author, year and title on new items; add missing DOIs (5 minutes).
3. **Merge duplicates** (2 minutes).
4. **Tag.** Add a status, a design and one or two themes (5 minutes).
5. **Write missing notes** for papers marked read without a note (10 minutes).
6. **Pick next week's reading** from the to-read saved search, no more than you can finish (3 minutes).

### A worked example

*This is an illustrative example with invented figures.* A master's student writing a thesis on exercise and depression in older adults has 212 items after three months. She creates 4 collections (Ch. 1 Intro, Ch. 2 Literature review, Ch. 3 Methods, Background). The duplicate check finds 17 pairs, so she merges them and has 195 items. Her status tags show 120 to read, 48 skimmed and 27 read. Twenty-seven notes exist, one for each read paper. At five read papers a week, the to-read list is 24 weeks long, so she uses the [levels of evidence](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) order to move the 9 systematic reviews and 31 randomized trials to the top and tags the remaining 80 `later`.

## FAQ

### How do researchers organize papers?

Most keep every paper in one reference manager, so each PDF is attached to a record with its author, year, title and DOI. They file papers in a few broad collections (one per project or chapter), use tags for themes, methods and reading status, and write a short note on each paper they read. A weekly session keeps the inbox empty.

### Should I use folders or tags for research papers?

Use both, for different jobs. Folders (collections) answer where a paper belongs: a project, a chapter, a review. Tags answer what a paper is about or what state it is in: a method, a theme, to-read. In Zotero a paper can sit in several collections without being copied, so you rarely need to choose one folder.

### How should I name research paper PDFs?

Use first author, year and a short title, separated by a consistent mark, for example: Smith - 2021 - Exercise and depression in older adults.pdf. Files then sort by author and stay readable outside your software. Zotero 7 renames attached files this way by default, so you rarely have to type names yourself.

### What is the best way to store PDFs for research?

Store each PDF attached to its record in a reference manager, not as a loose file in a downloads folder. Keep the library synced to at least one other place and back it up. Never keep two copies of the same paper: annotations split between them and you lose track of which one you cited.

### How do I keep track of research papers I have read?

Give every paper a reading status (to read, skimmed, read, cited) as a tag or field, and write a five-line note when you finish: question, method, main finding with page number, limits, and how you will use it. A saved search for the to-read tag gives you a live reading list that updates itself.
