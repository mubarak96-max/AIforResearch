# Quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods research: how they differ and how to choose

*Last updated: 14 November 2026*

Quantitative research collects numbers and uses statistics to measure how much, how often or how strongly things are related. Qualitative research collects words, images and observations and interprets them to explain how and why things happen and what they mean to people. Mixed methods research does both in one study and integrates the results. You choose between them from your research question: count and test, explore and explain, or both.

This guide compares the three on data, sampling, analysis, strengths and limits, explains the three core mixed methods designs, and ends with a way to justify your choice. It is part of our [types of research design](/blog/types-of-research-design) series.

## What is quantitative research?

**Quantitative research measures variables with structured instruments and analyzes the numbers with statistics to describe, compare or test relationships.** It usually starts from a hypothesis drawn from theory or earlier studies (deductive reasoning), and it aims to generalize from a sample to a population.

- **Data:** counts, scores, measurements, ratings, records. Each variable has an [operational definition](/blog/operational-definition-of-variables) that says exactly how it is measured.
- **Typical designs:** experiments, quasi-experiments, surveys, correlational and cohort studies.
- **Output:** estimates with confidence intervals, effect sizes, test results.

**Example (illustrative), business:** "Is the number of days worked from home per week associated with job satisfaction scores among 500 software developers, after adjusting for age and seniority?"

## What is qualitative research?

**Qualitative research collects open-ended, non-numerical data and interprets it to understand experiences, meanings, processes and contexts.** It often works inductively, building ideas from the data rather than testing a prediction, and it aims for depth and transferability, not statistical generalization.

- **Data:** interview and focus group transcripts, field notes, documents, images, open survey answers.
- **Typical designs:** case study, ethnography, grounded theory, phenomenology, narrative research, qualitative description.
- **Output:** themes, categories, a theory or model, a rich description, supported by quotations.

**Example (illustrative), health:** "How do community health workers in rural districts describe the barriers they face when following up patients who miss tuberculosis treatment?"

## What is mixed methods research?

**Mixed methods research collects and analyzes both quantitative and qualitative data within one study or program and integrates them, so the combination answers more than either strand would alone.** Johnson, Onwuegbuzie and Turner reviewed definitions from leading methodologists and found that integration of the two kinds of data, not just their presence, is what defines it [1].

- **Data:** both, with a plan for where they meet.
- **Integration:** by merging results, by using one phase to build or select for the next, or by embedding one inside the other [2].
- **Output:** findings from each strand plus a combined interpretation, often shown in a joint display (a table with numbers and themes side by side).

**Example (illustrative), education:** a survey of 600 students measures how often they use the library's study skills service and their grades; interviews with 20 frequent and 20 rare users then explain why some never come.

## Quantitative vs qualitative vs mixed methods: comparison table

**The three approaches differ in the question they answer, the data they collect, how they choose participants, how they analyze and how quality is judged.** Creswell and Creswell's *Research Design* is the standard textbook comparison of the three [7].

| | Quantitative | Qualitative | Mixed methods |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question | How many, how much, is X related to Y, does X cause Y | How, why, what is it like | Questions with both parts, or one part that needs the other |
| Logic | Mostly deductive: test a hypothesis | Mostly inductive: build understanding | Both, in a planned sequence or in parallel |
| Data | Numbers from structured measures | Words, images, observation | Both |
| Sampling | Probability sampling where possible; size from a power calculation | Purposive, theoretical or snowball; size from depth and saturation | Different sampling for each strand, linked by design |
| Typical size (illustrative) | Tens to thousands | About 5 to 50 | One of each |
| Analysis | Descriptive and inferential statistics | Coding, thematic analysis, constant comparison | Each strand analyzed, then integrated |
| Quality criteria | Reliability, validity, statistical power | Credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability | Both, plus quality of integration |
| Researcher's role | Kept at a distance from data | Part of the instrument; reflexivity expected | Both roles |
| Main strength | Precision, comparison, generalization | Depth, context, explaining process | Completeness; each strand covers the other's blind spot |
| Main limit | Misses context and reasons | Small samples; not built to estimate how common something is | Time, skills and length; integration is hard |

## How does sampling differ?

**Quantitative studies aim for a sample that represents a population and is large enough to detect the effect of interest; qualitative studies choose people who can best inform the question, and stop when more data adds little.**

For quantitative work, the sampling method decides how far you can generalize. A simple random or stratified sample supports claims about the population; a convenience sample does not. Our guide to [probability vs non-probability sampling](/blog/probability-vs-non-probability-sampling) covers each method. The number comes from a power calculation: see [statistical power and sample size](/blog/statistical-power-and-sample-size).

For qualitative work, purposive sampling picks participants with the experience you study, sometimes chosen to vary on key traits (maximum variation). Grounded theory uses theoretical sampling: the next participant is chosen to test or fill out the emerging theory. On size, Guest, Bunce and Johnson found that in 60 interviews with a fairly similar group of women, most of the codes they would ever find had appeared within the first 12 [3]. Malterud, Siersma and Guassora's information power model is a more flexible guide: you need fewer participants when the aim is narrow, the sample highly specific, theory is used and the interviews are rich [4].

**Example (illustrative), engineering:** a study of how site engineers decide when to stop a concrete pour in bad weather interviews 14 engineers chosen from large and small firms and from both precast and in-situ work, stopping when new interviews stop adding new decision rules.

## How does analysis differ?

**Quantitative analysis summarizes and tests numbers with statistics; qualitative analysis codes data into themes or categories and interprets them.**

| Step | Quantitative | Qualitative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prepare | Clean, code missing values, check distributions | Transcribe, read and re-read, write memos |
| Describe | Means, SDs, frequencies, charts | Initial codes on segments of text |
| Analyze | t-tests, ANOVA, chi-square, regression | Group codes into themes or categories; compare cases |
| Report | Tables, effect sizes, confidence intervals | Themes with quotations; a model or narrative |

For choosing a test, see [which statistical test to use](/blog/which-statistical-test-to-use). For coding, see [how to code qualitative data](/blog/how-to-code-qualitative-data); reflexive thematic analysis as set out by Braun and Clarke is the most common approach [5].

## What are the main mixed methods designs?

**Creswell and Plano Clark's third edition sets out three core designs: convergent, explanatory sequential and exploratory sequential** [6]. Earlier editions listed more designs (such as embedded and multiphase), which the third edition treats as complex designs built on the three core ones. <!-- VERIFY: wording of how the 3rd edition (2018) groups the earlier embedded, transformative and multiphase designs as "complex designs" -->

A common shorthand writes the strands in capitals for the priority strand, with an arrow for sequence and a plus for "at the same time": QUAN → qual, or QUAL + QUAN.

### Convergent design

**Collect quantitative and qualitative data in the same phase, analyze each separately, then merge the results to compare them.**

- **Use it when** you want two views of the same question and have the time and skills to run both at once.
- **Example (illustrative), health services:** in one month, 300 patients rate a new online booking system on a satisfaction scale, while 20 patients and 10 receptionists are interviewed. A joint display shows where the ratings and the themes agree or conflict.
- **Watch for:** what you will do if the two sets of results disagree. Plan to explain it, not hide it.

### Explanatory sequential design

**Run a quantitative phase first, then a qualitative phase to explain its results, often choosing interviewees from the survey.**

- **Use it when** you expect numbers to need explaining: a surprising result, outliers, or differences between groups.
- **Example (illustrative), education:** a survey of 900 secondary teachers finds that those in rural schools use a new digital platform much less. The researcher then interviews 16 rural teachers, chosen from high and low users, to find out why.
- **Watch for:** the time needed between phases, and deciding which results to follow up.

### Exploratory sequential design

**Run a qualitative phase first, then use its findings to build something (a survey, a scale, an intervention) that a quantitative phase tests.**

- **Use it when** no good instrument exists for your population or concept, or you need to know what matters before you measure it.
- **Example (illustrative), social science:** focus groups with gig-economy delivery riders identify seven sources of work stress. These become items in a new questionnaire, which is piloted with 350 riders and checked for reliability and validity.
- **Watch for:** the instrument-building step takes longer than most students plan for. See [reliability vs validity](/blog/reliability-vs-validity).

| Design | Order | Typical aim | Integration point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Convergent | Same time | Compare or corroborate | Merging results in analysis |
| Explanatory sequential | QUAN → qual | Explain results | Quantitative results select participants and shape questions |
| Exploratory sequential | QUAL → quan | Build and test an instrument or intervention | Qualitative findings become items, categories or a program |

## How do you choose between quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods?

**Choose from the question first, then check feasibility: quantitative if you need to count, compare or test; qualitative if you need to explore, explain or understand experience; mixed methods if your question has both parts and you have time to integrate them.**

| If your question… | Choose |
| --- | --- |
| Asks how many, how much, or whether groups differ | Quantitative |
| Tests a hypothesis about cause or association | Quantitative |
| Asks how people experience, understand or do something | Qualitative |
| Concerns a topic with little prior research or no good measure | Qualitative, or exploratory sequential |
| Needs numbers explained | Explanatory sequential |
| Needs a new instrument for a specific group | Exploratory sequential |
| Asks the same thing from two angles, for a fuller answer | Convergent |

Then check three things:

1. **Time.** Sequential designs need two phases of ethics, recruitment and analysis. A one-year master's often cannot fit both.
2. **Skills.** You need to do both statistics and qualitative analysis well. Supervisors can tell when one strand is an afterthought.
3. **Word count.** Mixed methods theses run longer. Check the limit before you commit.

## How do you justify your choice in a methodology chapter?

**State the approach, link it to your research question, explain what it lets you claim, and name the alternative you rejected and why.** Cite a methods text and one or two studies in your field that used the same approach.

**Example justification (illustrative):** "This study uses an explanatory sequential mixed methods design [6]. The first research question asks whether rural and urban teachers differ in their use of the platform, which requires a survey large enough to compare groups. The second asks why any difference exists, which a survey cannot answer in depth. Interviews with teachers selected from the survey results allow the qualitative phase to explain the quantitative findings. A convergent design was rejected because the interview sample could not be chosen until the survey results were known."

The [methodology chapter guide](/blog/how-to-write-a-methodology-chapter) shows where this paragraph goes and what follows it.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing qualitative because "I am not good at statistics".** Qualitative analysis is just as demanding. Choose from the question.
- **Calling a survey with two open questions "mixed methods".** Without a plan to integrate the strands, it is a survey.
- **Reporting qualitative findings as percentages.** "65% of participants said…" from 20 interviews suggests a precision the design does not have.
- **Using the wrong quality criteria.** Do not judge interviews by statistical power, or a survey by saturation.
- **Leaving integration to the discussion.** Say in your methods where and how the strands meet.

## FAQ

### What are the three types of research methods?

The three broad types are quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods. Quantitative research collects numbers and tests relationships or differences with statistics. Qualitative research collects words, images or observations and interprets meaning, experience and process. Mixed methods research collects both and integrates them in one study, so that each strand helps explain, test or build on the other. Your research question decides which you need.

### What is the main difference between qualitative and quantitative research?

Quantitative research asks how much, how many or how strongly, and answers with numbers from structured measures and larger samples. Qualitative research asks how, why or what it is like, and answers with themes or theory built from open-ended data from smaller, purposively chosen samples. The difference is in the question, the data and the kind of claim you can make, not in which is more scientific.

### Is a survey quantitative or qualitative?

A survey with closed questions, such as rating scales and multiple choice, is quantitative because the answers are counted and analyzed statistically. A survey with only open-ended questions produces qualitative data. Many surveys mix both. If you analyze the open answers with thematic analysis and integrate them with the numbers, you may have a small mixed methods study, but only if the integration is planned.

### What are the three core mixed methods designs?

Creswell and Plano Clark describe three core designs. In a convergent design, quantitative and qualitative data are collected in the same phase and merged. In an explanatory sequential design, a quantitative phase comes first and a qualitative phase explains its results. In an exploratory sequential design, a qualitative phase comes first and its findings shape a quantitative phase, such as a new survey instrument.

### How many participants do I need for qualitative research?

There is no fixed number. Sample size in qualitative research is judged by whether you have enough data to answer the question in depth. Guest and colleagues found most themes appeared within the first 12 interviews in a fairly homogeneous sample. Malterud and colleagues' information power model says you need fewer participants when the aim is narrow, the sample is specific and the dialogue is rich.
