# Free AI writing assistant: what you really get for free (and where it stops)

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

You can get a capable free AI writing assistant today: Grammarly Free for grammar, ChatGPT or Claude Free for drafting, Wordtune or QuillBot for rewrites, and WriteOrbit Free for cited writing. Each stops somewhere: daily caps, no citation styles, no version history, or privacy defaults you need to change. Here is exactly where.

This is part of our guide to the [best AI writing assistant for writers who need real sources](/blog/best-ai-writing-assistant). That page compares paid plans. This one covers only what you get without paying.

"Unlimited" on a free plan almost always means "unlimited basic use." The useful part, the better model, the long upload, the research mode, is the part that's metered.

## What free AI writing tools leave out

Three things are missing from almost every free plan, and they are the three that matter most for work you publish.

### Source checking

A free chatbot will happily write "a 2022 study found that 64% of readers…" without a source, or with one it made up. Studies of ChatGPT-generated references found high fabrication rates: 55% of GPT-3.5's citations and 18% of GPT-4's in one 2023 test.[8] Web search helps, because it returns links you can click, but not every answer triggers a search.

The free fix is manual: open every source before you keep a claim. Our [step-by-step guide to fact-checking AI output](/blog/how-to-fact-check-ai) covers how.

### Citation styles

Chat tools return citations as plain text in whatever format they guess. If you need APA 7, Chicago or a publisher's style, you retype them or run them through a separate formatter. Our guide to [AI citation generators](/blog/ai-citation-generator) compares the tools that format citations with the few that find the source as well.

### Version history and tracked changes

When an AI rewrites your paragraph in a chat window, there is no record of what it changed. Paste the result back into your draft and the original is gone. For anything an editor will review, you want suggestions (tracked changes) and version history. Chat tools and rewriters don't have them on any plan; document editors do.

## Privacy: is your draft used for training?

Free plans are where vendors are most likely to use your content to improve their models. Before you paste an unpublished story, a client report or a script:

| Tool | Default for free users | How to opt out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ChatGPT | New conversations can be used for training | Settings > Data controls > turn off "Improve the model for everyone." Temporary Chats aren't used for training.[9] |
| Claude | Consumer users choose whether chats are used to improve models; if allowed, data is kept up to five years | Privacy settings in your account[10] |
| Grammarly | Individual accounts can have content used for product improvement and training | Turn off the Product Improvement and Training control[11] |
| Others | Read the privacy policy and look for "train," "improve our models," and "retention" | Varies |

What to check in any tool's terms:

- [ ] Is my content used to train or "improve" models? Is that on by default?
- [ ] Can I turn it off, and does that apply to past content or only new content?
- [ ] How long is my content kept after I delete it?
- [ ] Do third-party AI providers the tool uses get to train on my content?
- [ ] Does giving feedback (thumbs up or down) change any of the above? On ChatGPT, rating a response can make that conversation available for training even with the setting off.[9]

## A free workflow for a 1,500-word sourced article

This uses only free plans. It takes longer than a paid workflow, mostly in step 4.

1. **Outline in a free chatbot.** Ask ChatGPT or Claude: "Outline a 1,500-word article on [topic] for [audience]. List the claims each section will need to support." Keep the claims list.
2. **Find sources before you draft.** For each claim, find a primary source: the study, the dataset, the official report. In WriteOrbit Free, search 250M+ scholarly works and save the papers. On the open web, use Google Scholar and the publisher's page. Note the DOI or URL.
3. **Draft in a document editor, not a chat window.** Write the article yourself, or use a few daily AI autocompletes for transitions. Cite as you go (in WriteOrbit, type `@` to cite a paper).
4. **Check every claim.** Open each source and confirm it says what your sentence says. Treat any number, quote or study that came from the chatbot as unconfirmed until you've seen it in the source.
5. **Polish with a rewriter.** Run tight paragraphs through Wordtune or QuillBot, or let Grammarly Free fix grammar. Re-read any rewritten sentence that contains a fact: rewrites can change meaning.
6. **Run one review pass.** WriteOrbit Free includes 3 review checks a day: use one for a claim check on the finished draft. Results appear as comments next to each claim.
7. **Export.** Download Word or PDF.

The free-tier bottleneck is daily caps. Five chat messages or ten rewrites go quickly on a long piece, so plan to spread the work over a couple of days or combine tools.

## When to upgrade: signs you've outgrown a free plan

Pay when one of these is true, not before:

- **You hit the daily cap before lunch** on most working days.
- **You spend more time retyping citations** than writing. A tool with built-in styles pays for itself fast.
- **An editor or client reviews your drafts** and you need tracked changes and version history that free chat tools don't offer.
- **You work with long PDFs** (court filings, annual reports, studies) and the free upload limit stops you.
- **You need a format the free plan doesn't export**, such as LaTeX.
- **Your work is confidential** and you need contractual privacy terms, which usually come with business plans.

## FAQ

### Is there a free AI writing assistant with no sign-up?

A few tools work without an account, with tighter limits. OpenAI's help center describes settings for signed-out ChatGPT users, so basic chat works logged out. Most other tools, including Claude, Grammarly and WriteOrbit, need a free account. An account also gives you saved history and privacy settings you can control, which matters more than skipping a sign-up form.

### Is ChatGPT free for writing?

Yes. ChatGPT has a free plan that you can use for drafting, outlining and rewriting, with limits on advanced models, uploads and research features. It doesn't format citations or keep tracked changes. Turn off "Improve the model for everyone" in Data controls if you don't want your drafts used for training, and check every fact or source it gives you.

### Is there a free AI writing assistant for Word?

Yes. Grammarly's free plan works inside Microsoft Word through the free Grammarly desktop app on Windows and Mac. It covers grammar, spelling and a monthly allowance of AI prompts. Another free route is a web editor that opens .docx files and exports them back to Word, such as WriteOrbit's free plan, which includes Word export.

### What is the best free AI writing assistant?

It depends on the job. For grammar and clarity everywhere you type, Grammarly Free. For drafting and brainstorming, the free plans of ChatGPT or Claude. For quick sentence rewrites, Wordtune or QuillBot. For writing that has to cite real research, WriteOrbit Free, which includes unlimited citations in every style. Many writers combine two.

### Do free AI writing assistants use my writing for training?

Some do by default. ChatGPT consumer accounts and Grammarly individual accounts both have settings that let you opt out of training. Claude asks consumer users to choose. Read each tool's data settings before you paste confidential material, and assume anything pasted into a free tool could be stored by the vendor.
