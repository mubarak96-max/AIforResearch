# The 15 best AI tools for students in 2026, by job

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

The best AI tools for students depend on the job: Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar to find sources, Gemini Notebook to study from your own notes, ChatGPT study mode or Khanmigo for guided tutoring, Grammarly to check writing, Zotero for references and Otter.ai for lectures. Most have free plans. Check your course's AI policy before using any of them for graded work.

This guide is for high school, undergraduate and graduate students choosing AI study tools. It is part of our guide to the [best AI for research](/blog/best-ai-for-research).

## How we chose these AI tools for students

**We picked tools that do one student job well, have a free plan or a student price, and point back to real sources where sources matter.** Facts come from each vendor's own pages, checked November 2026. We did not run a scored test, so there are no ratings; check the linked page before you pay, because student prices change each term.

## AI tools for research and finding sources

### 1. AI for Research

**Good at:** research assignments from question to cited draft. Find Papers searches 250M+ scholarly works and filters by study type, year and open access (0 credits). Deep Research gives a short cited answer for 1 credit. The editor formats citations in 10,000+ styles, and Slides makes a deck with speaker notes and a references slide.

**Free plan:** 45 credits a month. Plus is $12 a month for 750 credits. Details in the section below.

### 2. Google Scholar, with Scholar Labs

**Good at:** the widest free academic search. Scholar Labs, its AI search mode, breaks a detailed question into parts and explains how each paper helps; Google says it is available to all signed-in users [1]. Use "Cited by" to find newer papers on the same question.

**Free:** yes. Full guide: [Google Scholar AI](/blog/google-scholar-ai).

### 3. Semantic Scholar

**Good at:** clean, free paper search. It is run by Ai2, a non-profit, and indexes over 200 million papers [2].

**Free:** yes, with no paid tier [2].

### 4. Consensus

**Good at:** yes-or-no questions answered from studies, such as "Does sleep deprivation reduce exam performance?" The free plan includes 10 Pro messages and 3 Deep Searches a month, and students get 40% off Pro for 12 months with a verified student email [3].

### 5. Perplexity

**Good at:** fast answers with sources listed, useful for getting oriented. The free plan includes 3 Pro Searches a day; Education Pro costs $10 a month after SheerID verification and adds Learn Mode [4].

**Watch for:** sources are web pages; for a paper, cite the paper itself, not a page about it.

## AI tools for reading and reviewing

### 6. Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM)

**Good at:** studying from your own materials. Upload lecture slides, readings and notes, and it answers from those sources. Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook in July 2026 [5]. The free plan allows 100 notebooks and 50 sources per notebook [6].

**Tip:** make one notebook per course, and add each week's readings as they come.

### 7. Scholar PDF Reader

**Good at:** reading papers faster. This free Chrome extension turns in-text citations into links, adds AI outlines with a few bullets per section for selected English papers, and lets you highlight and comment [7][8].

## AI study tools for practice and tutoring

### 8. ChatGPT study mode

**Good at:** working through a problem step by step instead of being handed the answer. OpenAI's help center says study mode is available on ChatGPT plans globally, on web and mobile, and uses the same limits as normal chats [9]. <!-- VERIFY: openai.com refused automated access in November 2026; confirm plan availability and the menu name for study mode -->

**Use it for:** "Quiz me on the Krebs cycle, one question at a time, and don't give the answer until I try."

### 9. Gemini for students

**Good at:** study plans and practice. Google's student hub lets you start a study notebook, make flashcards and take practice quizzes [10]. Eligible US college students can claim a free year of Google AI Pro, and students in over 140 other markets a free year of Google AI Plus; redeem by 31 December 2026, and the plan bills monthly afterwards unless you cancel [10].

### 10. Khanmigo

**Good at:** patient tutoring across math, science, coding, history and writing. It guides you toward answers rather than giving them. Learners pay $4 a month or $44 a year; you must be 18 or older in the US, or be added by a parent [11].

### 11. Wolfram|Alpha

**Good at:** math, physics and chemistry with worked steps. Step-by-step solutions need Pro; Pro for Students is $5 a month, billed $60 a year [12]. The free version still computes answers.

**Ask first:** many universities require permission to record lectures.

## Is using AI cheating? How to use AI without breaking the rules

**Using AI is cheating when it breaks your course's rules or when you present AI work as your own; using it to learn is usually allowed.** Rules differ by course, so the first step is always the syllabus. UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education recommends an age limit for independent use and an age-appropriate approach [16]. Our guide to [AI in academic research policies](/blog/ai-in-academic-research-policies) covers university and journal rules.

Follow these five rules on any assignment:

1. **Read the policy for this course.** "Allowed for brainstorming, not for writing" is common. If it is unclear, email your instructor before you start.
2. **Do the thinking yourself.** Use AI to explain, quiz and check. Write the argument in your own words.
3. **Check every source.** Look up each reference; AI tools can produce citations to papers that don't exist.
4. **Disclose when asked.** Say which tool you used and for what, in the form your course requests.
5. **Keep your trail.** Save notes, drafts and search history so you can show how the work developed.

*Example (invented).* An instructor allows AI "for feedback, not drafting". A student writes a 1,500-word essay, asks Grammarly for clarity edits, asks ChatGPT study mode to question her argument, and adds one line at the end: "I used Grammarly for language edits and ChatGPT to test my argument; all text and sources are my own." That follows the rule. Pasting in an AI-written paragraph would not.

## A stack for a typical semester

**Set up the tools in week one, use them weekly, and save heavy research work for assignment weeks.**

1. **Week 1:** install Zotero and the Zotero browser connector. Make one Gemini notebook per course and upload the syllabus and slides.
2. **Every week:** record lectures with Otter.ai (where allowed) and add the transcript to the course notebook. Ask the notebook five practice questions from that week's material.
3. **Problem sets:** work through them in ChatGPT study mode, Khanmigo or Wolfram|Alpha, and attempt each step before asking for the next.
4. **Research essays:** search in Google Scholar or AI for Research, save each paper to Zotero or the Library, read the key ones in full, and write with citations attached.
5. **Before you submit:** run a grammar pass in Grammarly and check every reference.
6. **Exam weeks:** generate practice quizzes from your notebook and redo every question you got wrong.

## FAQ

### What AI tools can students use?

Students commonly use AI tools to find sources (Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, Consensus), read and question their own notes (Gemini Notebook), study with guided tutoring (ChatGPT study mode, Khanmigo), check writing (Grammarly), manage references (Zotero) and transcribe lectures (Otter.ai). Which ones you may use for graded work depends on your course's AI policy, so read it first and ask when it is unclear.

### Is using AI cheating?

It depends on your course's rules and on how you use it. Using AI to explain a concept, quiz you or find sources is usually allowed. Submitting AI-written text as your own work is usually treated as misconduct unless the assignment permits it. Read the policy for each course, disclose AI use when asked, keep your drafts and notes, and check every source an AI gives you.

### What is the best free AI tool for students?

For research, Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar are free with no paid tier. For studying, ChatGPT's study mode is available on its free plan, and Gemini Notebook's free plan allows 100 notebooks of up to 50 sources each. Zotero is free for references. Eligible college students can also claim a free year of a Google AI plan until 31 December 2026.

### Can teachers tell if you used AI?

Sometimes, and not reliably by software alone. Teachers notice references that do not exist, facts that do not match the sources, a sudden change in style, and work you cannot explain in your own words. The safest approach is to follow your course policy, disclose any permitted AI use, and be able to show your notes, sources and drafts.

### Which AI is best for writing essays as a student?

Use AI to plan, find sources and check your writing, not to write the essay for you. Grammarly's free plan checks grammar and includes 100 AI prompts a month. For research essays, a tool that finds real papers and keeps citations attached, such as AI for Research, prevents invented references. Write the argument yourself, then check every citation.
