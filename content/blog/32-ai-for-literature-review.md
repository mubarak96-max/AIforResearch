# AI for literature review: a 6-step method that keeps you in control

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

Use AI for literature review work to speed up the mechanical steps: finding papers, reading them with page citations, filling a synthesis matrix and drafting paragraphs from that matrix. Keep the judgment for yourself: the question, which studies count, what the evidence means and the final wording. Check every AI claim against the cited page, and disclose how you used AI.

This guide is for students and researchers writing a review chapter or article who want to use AI without losing control of the result. It is part of our series on literature reviews; the pillar guide, [how to write a literature review](/blog/how-to-write-a-literature-review), covers structure, synthesis and the matrix template in full. If you want a comparison of tools rather than a method, see [AI literature review tools](/blog/ai-literature-review-tools).

## Can AI do a literature review?

AI for literature review work can do about half the work of a literature review, the half that is searching, sorting and extracting. It cannot do the half that examiners mark. **An AI literature review tool is software that searches scholarly papers, extracts information from them and drafts text with citations back to each paper.**

| Step | What AI does well | What stays with you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | Suggests synonyms and sub-questions | Choosing the question and scope |
| Search | Finds papers that use different words for the same idea | Choosing databases; searching your field's own database |
| Screening | Ranks results against your criteria | Inclusion decisions |
| Reading | Answers questions about a PDF with page numbers | Judging study quality and relevance |
| Extraction | Fills the same fields for many papers | Checking every number you will quote |
| Synthesis | Drafts paragraphs from a matrix | The argument, the themes and the gap |
| Checking | Confirms references exist; flags retractions | Confirming each source supports its sentence |

The risk is specific. A general chatbot asked for a literature review can invent references: one study found 55% of GPT-3.5's citations and 18% of GPT-4's were fabricated, and many real ones had errors [1]. Even tools that cite real papers can overstate them: a 2025 study found language model summaries of scientific research were far more likely than human summaries to contain overgeneralized conclusions [2]. The method below is built around those two failures.

## Step 1: How do you set the question and search terms with AI?

Write the question yourself, then use AI to widen your vocabulary. Ask: "List synonyms, related terms and controlled vocabulary for each concept in this question." You get terms you would have missed; you decide which to keep.

*Example (invented):* question: "How does AI tutoring software affect mathematics achievement in secondary school students?" Concepts and terms:

| Concept | Terms to search |
| --- | --- |
| AI tutoring | intelligent tutoring system, adaptive learning, computer-assisted instruction |
| Mathematics achievement | math performance, test scores, learning outcomes |
| Secondary school | high school, middle school, adolescents, grades 6 to 12 |

Combine terms within a concept with OR and across concepts with AND. For building a full string, see [how to search PubMed](/blog/how-to-search-pubmed) or, for systematic work, [systematic review search strategy](/blog/systematic-review-search-strategy).

## Step 2: How do you find papers with AI search?

Run the same question in at least two places: an AI search tool for breadth and your field's database (ERIC, PsycINFO, PubMed, through your library) for coverage. AI search finds papers by meaning, which catches studies that use different terms; database search with controlled vocabulary catches what semantic search ranks low.

1. Run the AI search and save every plausible paper to one library folder.
2. Run the database search and add new papers to the same folder.
3. Pick the 3 to 5 strongest papers and snowball: check their references and who has cited them since. See [citation snowballing](/blog/citation-snowballing).
4. Record each search: tool, query, date, filters, number of results.

Stop when new searches mostly return papers you already have.

## Step 3: How do you read and extract with PDF chat?

Ask each PDF the same narrow questions and keep the page number with every answer. **PDF chat is a tool that answers questions about a document from its text and points to the page each answer came from.** It is fast and it fails in predictable ways, covered in [chat with PDF](/blog/chat-with-pdf).

Use the same five questions for every paper:

1. "What question does this study ask? Quote the aim with the page."
2. "What is the design, sample size and setting?"
3. "What is the main result, with the number? Give the page."
4. "What limitations do the authors state?"
5. "If the paper doesn't say, answer 'not stated'."

Open the cited page for every number you will quote. Read the abstract and discussion yourself for the papers your argument rests on; see [how to read a research paper](/blog/how-to-read-a-research-paper).

## Step 4: How do you build a synthesis matrix with AI?

Put one paper per row and one theme or field per column, let AI fill the cells, then correct them. The matrix is where synthesis starts: you read down a column and see the pattern. The full template and a filled example are in [how to write a literature review](/blog/how-to-write-a-literature-review).

Columns for the example above (*invented*):

| Study | Design, n | Outcome measure | Effect on achievement | Duration | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Study A, 2022 | RCT, 640 | State test | Small gain (p. 8) | 1 year | One district |
| Study B, 2023 | Quasi-experiment, 1,900 | Course grades | No difference (p. 12) | 1 term | Teacher-graded |
| Study C, 2024 | RCT, 310 | Standardized test | Gain for low achievers only (p. 7) | 6 months | Small subgroup |

Check a sample: open five random cells and compare them with the page. If more than one is wrong, check every cell in that column.

## Step 5: How do you draft themes with citations?

Draft from the matrix, not from a blank prompt. Write the claim for each paragraph yourself, then ask AI to draft supporting sentences from specific rows. A good instruction: "Using only studies A, B and C from this table, write a paragraph arguing that effects are larger in randomized trials than in studies using course grades. Cite each study."

Then rewrite. A drafted paragraph usually summarizes study by study; turn it into synthesis by leading with the claim and grouping the studies. The pillar guide shows [a summary paragraph and a synthesis paragraph side by side](/blog/how-to-write-a-literature-review).

## Step 6: How do you check every claim against its source?

Check two things for every citation: that the reference exists and that it supports the sentence. Missing papers are easy to catch; misattributed claims are not.

1. Run a citation check on the reference list for existence, retractions and wrong years or authors.
2. For each sentence with a citation, open the paper at the page and confirm it says what you say.
3. Watch for overgeneralization: "improves achievement" when the study found a gain only for one subgroup.
4. Recheck any number that came from AI extraction.

For the full method, see [how to check a claim against the research](/blog/how-to-check-a-claim-against-the-research).

## Is it ethical to use AI for a literature review?

Yes, when you follow your institution's rules, disclose your use and stay responsible for every claim. Publisher and ethics bodies agree on three points: AI cannot be an author because it cannot take responsibility; authors must disclose how they used it; and authors remain accountable for the content [3][4]. Universities set their own rules for coursework and theses, and they differ, so read yours. See [AI in academic research policies](/blog/ai-in-academic-research-policies).

A disclosure statement can be two sentences. *Example:* "I used AI for Research (November 2026) to search for papers and to extract study characteristics into a table. I checked all extracted data and every citation against the original papers, and wrote the synthesis myself."

## FAQ

### Can AI do a literature review?

AI can do parts of one well: suggest search terms, find papers, pull the same details from each paper into a table and draft paragraphs with citations. It cannot set your question, judge which studies are trustworthy, decide what the evidence means for your project or take responsibility for the result. Treat AI output as a draft you check against the papers, not as a finished review.

### Is it ethical to use AI for a literature review?

Yes, if you follow your institution's rules, disclose how you used it and check its output. Most university and publisher policies allow AI for search and editing support but hold you responsible for every claim and citation, and say AI cannot be an author. Using AI to write text you submit as your own without disclosure may breach academic integrity rules. Check your course or journal policy first.

### Does AI make up references in literature reviews?

General chatbots can. A 2023 study in Scientific Reports found that 55% of the citations GPT-3.5 produced for short literature reviews were fabricated, and 18% of GPT-4's. Tools that search a real index and cite what they retrieve avoid invented references, but can still cite a real paper for a claim it doesn't make. Check every reference exists and supports its sentence.

### What is the best AI tool for literature review?

Pick by step rather than by brand. You need a tool that searches a real scholarly index, one that answers questions about a PDF with page numbers, a way to extract the same fields from many papers, and a citation and claim check at the end. Some products cover several steps. Our comparison of AI literature review tools covers Elicit, Consensus, SciSpace, Scite and others.

### How do I disclose AI use in a literature review?

Say which tool you used, its version or date, and what you used it for: searching, summarizing, extracting or editing. Say that you checked its output against the sources. Put it where your institution or journal asks, usually the methods section or acknowledgments. Do not list AI as an author. Keep your prompts and outputs in case your supervisor or editor asks.
