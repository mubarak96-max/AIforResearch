# The best AI writing assistant for writers who need real sources (2026)

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

The best AI writing assistant is the one whose facts you can check. For polishing prose, Grammarly is hard to beat. For drafting and brainstorming, ChatGPT and Claude lead. For marketing copy at scale, Jasper. For writing that must cite real research, such as reports, science journalism and white papers, pick a tool that links every claim to a source you can open.

This guide ranks tools on that test: can you verify what they write? It is the pillar page for our series on AI writing tools. If money is the main constraint, start with [what free AI writing assistants really give you](/blog/free-ai-writing-assistant).

## What an AI writing assistant does in 2026

An AI writing assistant is software that uses a large language model to help you draft, rewrite, check or research text inside your writing workflow. In 2026 most tools do some mix of four jobs:

1. **Autocomplete.** The tool suggests the next phrase, sentence or paragraph as you type. Quality varies most here: a suggestion that sounds right is not the same as one that is right.
2. **Rewriting.** You select text and ask for a shorter, clearer, more formal or friendlier version. Grammarly, QuillBot and Wordtune began here.
3. **Chat.** You ask questions in a side panel or a separate window: "outline this," "what's missing," "summarize this PDF." ChatGPT and Claude are the best known.
4. **Review.** The tool reads your finished draft and leaves feedback: grammar, tone, structure, and in a few tools, whether the claims are supported.

The fifth job, the one that decides whether you can publish what the tool writes, is **sourcing**: showing where a fact came from. Most assistants still don't do it, or do it only when they run a web search.

## The hallucination problem: AI errors that ended in public corrections

An AI hallucination is a confident statement from an AI model that is false or unsupported, such as an invented quote, statistic or citation. Language models predict likely text. A plausible author name, journal title and year are likely text, whether or not the paper exists.

The research is clear about how often this happens with citations. In a 2023 study in *Scientific Reports*, Walters and Wilder asked ChatGPT to write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references by hand. **55% of GPT-3.5's citations and 18% of GPT-4's were fabricated**, and many of the real ones had substantive errors.[1] A 2023 study in *Cureus* of medical articles written by ChatGPT-3.5 found that 47% of 115 references were fabricated and only 7% were both real and accurate.[2] Newer models do better, but no published test has shown the problem gone.

These are not lab curiosities. Each case below is documented and ended in a public correction, sanction or refund:

| Year | Who | What went wrong | What happened next |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2023 | Lawyers in *Mata v. Avianca* (U.S. federal court, New York) | A brief cited court decisions that did not exist, produced with ChatGPT. The judge described "bogus judicial decisions with bogus quotes and bogus internal citations." | The court sanctioned the lawyers in June 2023, including a $5,000 penalty.[3] |
| 2023 | CNET | An in-house AI tool helped write 77 personal-finance explainers. An internal audit found errors in 41 of them. | CNET corrected the stories with editor's notes and paused the tool.[4][5] |
| 2025 | Chicago Sun-Times (syndicated insert) | A summer reading list in a licensed special section included books that do not exist, attributed to real authors. The freelance writer said he had used AI. | The paper apologized and said the section was not produced by its newsroom.[6] |
| 2025 | Deloitte Australia | An AU$440,000 government report contained fabricated references and an invented quote from a court judgment. Deloitte said parts were produced with a generative AI tool chain. | A corrected report was published and Deloitte agreed to a partial refund.[7] |
| 2025 | U.S. "MAHA" health report | Reporters found cited studies that could not be located; named researchers said they had not written them. | The White House posted a revised version with the citations replaced. It blamed "formatting issues" and did not confirm AI use.[8] |

Two lessons run through all five. First, the errors were the kind that look right: real authors, real journals, plausible titles. Second, nobody caught them until someone tried to open the source. That is the test this guide uses.

## 6 criteria for choosing an AI writing assistant

Rank each tool on these six, in this order, if accuracy matters to your work.

### 1. Source grounding

Source grounding means the assistant's output is tied to specific documents you can open and check. Ask: when the tool states a fact, does it show where the fact came from? Is the source a real, open-able item (a DOI, a URL, a page in your PDF), or just a name and year?

**Test it:** ask for three statistics on a narrow topic you know well, with sources. Open every source. Count how many exist and how many say what the tool claims.

### 2. Citation styles

If you publish reports, books or academic work, you need real reference lists, not pasted links. Check which styles the tool supports (APA 7, MLA 9, Chicago, IEEE, Harvard, or a publisher's own), and whether you can switch style without retyping. Our [guide to AI citation generators](/blog/ai-citation-generator) explains the difference between tools that format citations and tools that find them.

### 3. Collaboration

An editor needs to see what changed and why. Look for real-time co-editing, comments, suggestions (tracked changes) and version history. Chat-only tools fail this test: the draft lives in a conversation, not a shared document.

### 4. Word compatibility

Most editors, clients and agencies still work in Microsoft Word. Either the assistant works inside Word, or it opens `.docx` files without wrecking their styles and exports clean Word files back.

### 5. Privacy

Find out whether your drafts train the vendor's models, and how to turn that off. Defaults differ:

- **ChatGPT:** consumer accounts have an "Improve the model for everyone" setting under Data controls. When it is off, new conversations aren't used for training. Business, Enterprise and Edu workspaces are not used for training by default.[9]
- **Claude:** since Anthropic's 2025 consumer-terms update, Free, Pro and Max users choose whether their chats are used to improve models. If you allow it, data is kept for up to five years; if not, the 30-day retention policy applies. Commercial and API use is not covered by that change.[10]
- **Grammarly:** individual Free and Pro users can opt out of their content being used for product improvement and training with the Product Improvement and Training control.[11]

For confidential material (unpublished investigations, client reports, scripts under NDA), also ask where the files are stored and who can reach them.

### 6. Price

Price last, because a cheap tool that invents one citation can cost more than a year's subscription. Compare what the free plan really allows per day or month, not just the headline price.

### Where each tool is strongest

- **Grammarly** is the best general-purpose checker because it follows you everywhere you type. Citation Finder is a real step toward sourcing, but it lives in Grammarly's own docs surface, and free use is capped at five inserted citations a day.[17]
- **ChatGPT** is the most flexible. With web search on, it shows linked sources you can click. The risk is the answer that doesn't trigger a search: it can still produce a confident reference from memory.
- **Claude** handles very long documents and editing passes well. Like ChatGPT, it is a chat tool first: your draft and its sources live in different places.
- **Jasper** is built for marketing teams: brand voice, campaigns, many assets. It is the most expensive tool here for one writer, and sourcing is not what it is for.
- **QuillBot and Wordtune** are rewriting tools. They are good at making a sentence shorter or clearer. They don't add facts, which also means they don't check them.

## Choosing an AI writing assistant by job

### News

Use AI for research and structure, not for facts it can't show you. Every newsroom policy we checked says the same: AP treats generative AI output as "unvetted source material," Reuters makes the journalist whose name is on the story responsible for it, and the Guardian requires an editor's sign-off for any generative AI use.[18][19][20] Never accept a quote from an assistant. Verify claims with an [AI fact checker](/blog/ai-fact-checker), then by hand. The [newsroom workflow guide](/blog/ai-tools-for-journalists) walks through each stage.

### Reports and white papers

Readers of a report check the references, and a single invented one damages the whole document. Choose a tool that cites real works and outputs a reference list in your required style. If you are building the evidence base first, see [AI literature review tools](/blog/ai-literature-review-tools). If your references live in Zotero or Mendeley, see [Zotero vs. Mendeley](/blog/zotero-vs-mendeley) and import them rather than retyping.

### Scripts

General assistants are useful for loglines, research and notes, and weak at screenplay formatting. Keep the script in dedicated screenwriting software. Use AI for research questions ("what did a 1970s newsroom sound like?") and check the answers against real sources.

### Books

Nonfiction authors need what report writers need: real citations, a consistent style (often Chicago), and a Word draft their editor can mark up. Fiction writers get most from chat tools for brainstorming and rewriters for line edits.

## A 10-minute test to run before you pay for any AI writing assistant

Run this on the free plan of each tool on your shortlist. Use a topic you know well.

```text
1. Ask: "Give me three statistics about [your topic], each with a source I can open."
   Score: how many sources exist? How many say what the tool claims?

2. Ask: "Cite two peer-reviewed studies on [narrow question], in APA 7."
   Score: look up each DOI at https://doi.org/. Does it resolve to that paper?

3. Paste one paragraph of your own draft. Ask: "Which claims here need a source?"
   Score: did it find the claims an editor would flag?

4. Ask it to shorten the same paragraph by 30%.
   Score: did any fact change or disappear?

5. Open the privacy or data settings.
   Score: can you stop your drafts from being used for training?
```

A tool that fails test 1 or 2 can still be a fine rewriting tool. It just isn't the one to trust with facts.

## FAQ

### Is there a free AI writing assistant?

Yes. Grammarly, ChatGPT, Claude, QuillBot, Wordtune and WriteOrbit all have free plans with daily or monthly limits. Jasper has a 7-day trial instead of a free plan. Free plans are good for short pieces and testing. They run out quickly on long, sourced work, and their privacy defaults differ, so check the training settings before you paste a draft in.

### Does Grammarly cite sources?

Partly. Grammarly's Citation Finder agent, which works in Grammarly's own docs editor, flags claims that may need a source, suggests sources and inserts citations in APA, MLA or Chicago. Free users can insert up to five citations a day. Grammarly's core checker in other apps fixes grammar, clarity and tone and does not look for sources.

### Can I use an AI writing assistant in Word?

Yes. Grammarly works inside Microsoft Word through its desktop app, and Anthropic offers a Claude add-in for Word. Another route is to write in a web editor that opens .docx files and exports back to Word, which is how WriteOrbit works. Check whether your organization allows add-ins before you install one.

### Which AI writing assistant is most accurate?

No AI writing assistant is accurate by default. Every tool built on a large language model can produce false statements. The more useful question is which tool lets you check its output fastest. Pick an assistant whose claims link to sources you can open, such as real papers, web pages or page numbers in your own PDFs, and check every one.

### What is the best AI writing assistant for journalists?

For drafting and research, journalists do best with a tool that shows its sources, keeps an edit trail for editors and works on a phone. Pair it with a separate grammar checker and your newsroom's style guide. Never let any assistant supply quotes, and follow your outlet's AI policy, as AP, Reuters and the Guardian all require human checking.
