# White paper vs research paper: differences and when to use each

*Last updated: 13 November 2026*

A research paper reports new findings to other researchers, and peer review checks it before a journal publishes it. A white paper usually reports no new study. It gathers existing evidence to persuade decision makers towards a policy, method or technology, and the organisation that wrote it publishes it without peer review. The research paper's job is to discover; the white paper's job is to recommend.

This article compares the two in detail, then sets the white paper against the other documents it is often confused with: the case study, the policy brief, the e-book and the report. If you are new to the form, start with [what is a white paper](/blog/what-is-a-white-paper).

## White paper vs research paper at a glance

| | Research paper | White paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Report new knowledge from a study | Inform a decision and recommend an approach |
| **Main question** | "What did we find?" | "What should you do, and why?" |
| **Reader** | Specialists in the field | Decision makers: officials, executives, buyers, funders, the informed public |
| **Evidence** | The authors' own data and analysis, set against prior research | Existing research, data and examples, synthesised; sometimes the publisher's own data |
| **Review** | Peer reviewed by independent experts before publication | Usually reviewed internally, if at all |
| **Publisher** | A journal or conference | The author's organisation: a company, agency, think tank or research group |
| **Structure** | IMRaD: introduction, methods, results, discussion | Executive summary, problem, evidence, approach, recommendations |
| **Tone** | Neutral, hedged, precise | Authoritative and persuasive, but evidence-led |
| **Ends with** | Conclusions and future research | Recommendations and a next step |
| **Length** | Set by the journal, often 3,000–8,000 words | Often 2,500–5,000 words for business papers; longer for policy and technical |
| **Cost to read** | Often behind a paywall unless open access | Usually free, sometimes behind an email form |
| **Who can cite it, and how** | Cited as evidence for findings | Cited as the publisher's position or synthesis (grey literature) |

## The differences in detail

### Purpose: discovering vs recommending

A research paper exists to add something new to knowledge: a result, a method or a theory. Its conclusions are limited to what the study can support, and it often ends by saying what further research is needed.

A white paper exists to move a reader towards a decision. It may not add any new knowledge at all; its value is in selecting, organising and explaining the evidence that bears on one problem, and in turning it into recommendations. That is why a white paper always ends with what to do next.

### Audience: specialists vs decision makers

A research paper is written for people who know the field. It can use the field's terms without explaining them, and its readers expect to see the method in enough detail to judge or repeat it.

A white paper is written for people who must act but are not specialists in the question: a health ministry reading about vaccine uptake, an IT director reading about a database architecture, a foundation reading about a research priority. It explains terms, leads with the conclusion, and puts detail in tables, appendices and references.

### Evidence and peer review

This is the difference that matters most for trust. A research paper's claims have been examined by independent reviewers before publication. A white paper's claims have been examined by whoever its publisher chose, if anyone, and the publisher often has an interest in the conclusion.

That does not make white papers unreliable, but it moves the burden onto the reader and the writer:

- **Readers** should check the sources behind the important claims, and notice when a figure comes only from the publisher's own customers or pilots.
- **Writers** should cite every number, separate independent evidence from their own results, say how strong the evidence is and describe alternatives fairly. These habits do for a white paper what peer review does for a research paper.

### Structure

A research paper uses the IMRaD structure (introduction, methods, results and discussion), with an abstract at the start. If you are writing one, see [how to write a research paper](/blog/how-to-write-a-research-paper).

A white paper is organised around the reader's decision. It opens with an executive summary and moves through the problem, the evidence, the approach and implementation to recommendations. The details are in [white paper format](/blog/white-paper-format).

| Research paper section | Closest white paper section | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abstract | Executive summary | The summary leads with the recommendation, not the method |
| Introduction | Introduction and the problem | Framed as a cost or risk to the reader, not a gap in the literature |
| Methods | "How this paper was prepared" (scientific papers) or absent | Short, or a note on sources |
| Results | What the evidence says | Many studies synthesised, not one study reported |
| Discussion | The approach; implementation | Turned into actions |
| Conclusion | Recommendations and next step | Specific, addressed to the reader |

### When a white paper and a research paper meet: the scientific white paper

The line blurs in science. A **scientific white paper** is written by researchers, cites peer-reviewed studies and may be published by a learned society or a research consortium. It is still a white paper, because its aim is to inform a decision: what a funder should prioritise, what an agency should regulate, what an industry should adopt. Community white papers for planning exercises, such as the decadal surveys in astronomy, are a clear case. <!-- VERIFY: Astro2020 solicited science white papers (also referenced in posts 125 and 126) -->

## White paper vs other documents

### White paper vs case study

| | Case study | White paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | One customer, project or event | A problem shared by many readers |
| Shape | A story: situation, action, result | An argument: problem, evidence, approach, recommendations |
| Evidence | The one case, often with a quote | Many sources, including case studies |
| Length | 1–3 pages | 6–12 pages or more |

Use a case study to prove that something worked once, in a setting like the reader's. Use a white paper to make the general case. Many good white papers include one or two short case studies as evidence, with the setting and sample stated.

### White paper vs policy brief

| | Policy brief | Policy white paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length | 2–4 pages | 15 pages or more |
| Reader | A busy official who needs to decide soon | Officials, legislators, stakeholders and the public who will examine the reasoning |
| Content | Key messages, the evidence in brief, options and a recommendation | The full case for change, options considered, detailed proposals, costs, implementation and consultation questions |
| Tone | Decision-ready | Thorough and open to response |

A policy brief is often written from a white paper, as its short version for ministers or legislators. See the [policy white paper template](/templates/policy-white-paper) for the long form.

### White paper vs e-book

| | E-book | White paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | A whole topic, often for beginners | One problem and one approach |
| Style | Light, visual, conversational | Formal, evidence-led |
| Evidence | Some | Every claim sourced |
| Typical stage | Early awareness | Consideration and decision |

Marketers sometimes publish the same material in both forms. If your document teaches broadly and has few sources, call it a guide or an e-book; readers who download a "white paper" expect evidence.

### White paper vs report

A report records information: what was done, what was found, what happened over a period. It may make recommendations, but it does not have to. A white paper always argues for something. A research or evaluation report that ends with "this is what you should do, and here is the evidence" is moving towards a white paper. For reports that inform an internal decision rather than persuade outside readers, see [how to write a research report](/blog/how-to-write-a-research-report).

## Which one should you write?

| If your aim is to… | Write a… |
| --- | --- |
| Publish the results of a study you carried out | Research paper |
| Persuade decision makers to adopt an approach, using existing evidence | White paper |
| Summarise the state of the research for a funder, agency or industry | Scientific white paper |
| Explain how a technology works and why it was designed that way | Technical white paper |
| Propose a change in law or policy, with options and costs | Policy white paper |
| Help a minister decide this week | Policy brief |
| Show one customer's result | Case study |
| Teach a broad topic to beginners | Guide or e-book |

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a white paper and a research paper?

A research paper reports new findings from a study the authors carried out, is written for specialists and is checked by peer review before a journal publishes it. A white paper usually reports no new study: it gathers existing evidence to support a recommendation, is written for decision makers, and is published by an organisation without peer review. One discovers; the other recommends.

### Is a white paper peer reviewed?

Usually not. A white paper is published by its author or organisation, who decides when it is ready. Some go through internal or expert review, and community science white papers may be read by a panel, but that is not the anonymous journal peer review a research paper receives. Readers should judge a white paper by its sources.

### Can a white paper be cited in academic work?

Yes, as grey literature, but with care. Cite it for what the organisation says or proposes, not as proof of a scientific finding, and prefer the primary studies the white paper relies on where they exist. In APA 7, cite it as a report with the description [White paper] after the title.

### Is a white paper the same as a report?

Not quite. A report can be purely descriptive, recording what happened or what was found. A white paper always argues for something: a policy, an approach, a technology or a research priority. Many white papers contain report-like sections, but they end with recommendations.

### What is the difference between a white paper and a case study?

A case study tells the story of one customer, project or event: the situation, what was done and the result. A white paper addresses a problem that affects many readers and argues for an approach to it, using evidence from many sources. Case studies are often used inside white papers as one piece of evidence.
