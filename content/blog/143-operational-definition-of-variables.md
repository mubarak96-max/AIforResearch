# Operational definition of variables

*Last updated: 13 November 2026*

An operational definition of a variable states exactly how you will measure it in your study: which indicator you observe, with which instrument or procedure, how it is scored, and in what unit or categories. It turns an abstract concept such as "stress" into something another researcher could measure the same way, such as "the total score (0 to 40) on the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale". Every variable in your hypotheses needs one, backed by evidence that the measure is reliable and valid.

This guide is part of our series on planning a study and thesis; start with [how to write a research question](/blog/how-to-write-a-research-question) if you have not fixed yours. Operational definitions come after the [hypothesis](/blog/how-to-write-a-hypothesis) and the framework, and before the [methodology chapter](/blog/how-to-write-a-methodology-chapter) is written.

## Conceptual vs operational definitions

| | Conceptual definition | Operational definition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Answers | What does the concept mean? | How will I measure it here? |
| Source | Theory and the literature, cited | Your design, instrument and scoring |
| Example | "Burnout is a state of emotional, physical and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged stress at work." | "Burnout is the score on the emotional exhaustion subscale of [named instrument], with higher scores meaning more exhaustion." |
| Where it goes | Literature review or definitions of terms | Methodology: variables and measures |

The two must line up. If your conceptual definition says burnout has three dimensions and your measure captures only one, say so and justify it, or change one of them. Mismatches here are a common examiner comment.

## The parts of a good operational definition

A complete operational definition answers six questions:

1. **Construct:** which concept, as defined conceptually.
2. **Indicator:** what observable thing stands for it (a score, a count, a behavior, a record).
3. **Instrument or procedure:** the questionnaire, test, device, observation protocol or data source.
4. **Scoring and unit:** how raw responses become a value; the range, unit or categories; what higher means.
5. **Level of measurement:** nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio.
6. **Evidence of quality:** reliability and validity, from the literature and from your own data.

## Levels of measurement

The level decides which statistics are meaningful and which tests you can run. Our guide to [which statistical test to use](/blog/which-statistical-test-to-use) starts from it.

| Level | Properties | Examples | Typical summary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominal** | Categories, no order | Sex, blood type, ward, country | Counts and percentages |
| **Ordinal** | Ordered categories, unequal steps | Education level, pain rated mild/moderate/severe, a single Likert item | Median, percentages |
| **Interval** | Equal steps, no true zero | Temperature in Celsius, many summed scale scores (by convention) | Mean, SD |
| **Ratio** | Equal steps, true zero | Income, age, reaction time, length of stay | Mean, SD, ratios |

Two practical notes. First, a single Likert item is ordinal; a summed score across many items is often treated as interval, which is common practice but worth stating as an assumption. Second, collect data at the highest level you can: you can turn age in years into age groups later, but not the reverse.

## Choosing indicators and instruments

**Indicators.** Most constructs can be measured in several ways: self-report, observation, records, physiological measures, or performance. Choose the one that matches your conceptual definition and your design. "Physical activity" as self-reported minutes per week and as accelerometer step counts are different variables.

**Instruments.** Prefer an existing, published instrument with evidence from a population like yours over writing your own items. When you choose one:

- Check that it measures your construct as you defined it.
- Check the evidence of reliability and validity, ideally in your population and language.
- Check the license. Many scales are free for research; some are copyrighted and need permission or a fee.
- If you translate or adapt it, follow a forward and back translation process and pilot it.
- If no instrument exists, write items from your conceptual definition, have experts review them for content validity, and pilot them.

## Reliability and validity evidence

Report the evidence for each measure in your methodology. The main kinds:

| Kind | Question it answers | Common evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal consistency | Do the items measure one thing? | Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega |
| Test-retest reliability | Is the score stable over time? | Correlation or ICC between two administrations |
| Inter-rater reliability | Do observers agree? | Cohen's kappa, ICC; see [Cohen's kappa](/blog/cohens-kappa-inter-rater-reliability) |
| Content validity | Do the items cover the concept? | Expert review, content validity index |
| Construct validity | Does it behave as theory predicts? | Factor analysis; convergent and discriminant correlations |
| Criterion validity | Does it agree with a standard or predict an outcome? | Correlation with a gold standard; sensitivity and specificity |

Cite published values from validation studies, and report reliability in your own sample too, for example the alpha you obtained.

## Worked examples across fields

*Example (invented for illustration).* Each row is a plausible operationalization, not a recommendation for your study. Instrument details and score ranges should be checked against the instrument's own manual and validation papers.

| Field | Construct | Conceptual definition (short) | Indicator and instrument | Scoring and level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Psychology | Perceived stress | The degree to which life feels unpredictable, uncontrollable and overloaded | Perceived Stress Scale, 10 items (PSS-10) | Sum 0 to 40; higher = more stress; treated as interval |
| Psychology | Self-esteem | A person's overall evaluation of their own worth | Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), 10 items | Sum 0 to 30 (with 0 to 3 coding); treated as interval |
| Nursing | Depressive symptoms | Frequency of depressive symptoms in the past two weeks | Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) | Sum 0 to 27; also severity bands (ordinal); a screening score, not a diagnosis |
| Nursing | Hand hygiene compliance | Performing hand hygiene at each required moment of care | Direct observation using a structured observation form | Performed actions divided by opportunities, as a percentage; ratio |
| Education | Math achievement | Mastery of the grade's math curriculum | End-of-term standardized test score | Scaled score; interval |
| Education | Class attendance | Presence at scheduled sessions | School attendance records | Sessions attended divided by sessions held; ratio |
| Economics | Household income | Total money income of all household members | Survey question on monthly income, in local currency | Amount per month; ratio (often log-transformed) |
| Economics | Financial inclusion | Access to formal financial services | Whether any household member holds an account at a formal institution | Yes / No; nominal |
| Engineering | System usability | Users' perceived ease of use of a system | System Usability Scale (SUS), 10 items | Score 0 to 100; treated as interval |
| Engineering | Response latency | Time for the system to respond to a request | Server-side timing logs over 10,000 requests | Milliseconds (median and 95th percentile); ratio |

Notice how the same field uses very different kinds of indicators: a scale, an observation, a record, a log. The quality evidence differs too: alpha for a scale, inter-rater agreement for observation, data completeness for records.

## An operationalization table template

Copy this into your methodology chapter. One row per variable, in the order they appear in your framework.

| Variable | Role | Conceptual definition (with citation) | Indicator | Instrument or source | Scoring and unit | Level | Reliability and validity evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Name] | IV / DV / mediator / moderator / control | [Definition] (Author, Year) | [What is observed] | [Instrument, version, items] | [Range, direction, unit] | [N/O/I/R] | [Published alpha or validation study; your sample's value] |

Then write a short paragraph under the table for each instrument: what it is, who developed it, how it is scored, its evidence, any adaptation, and its license.

## Step by step

1. **List every variable** in your hypotheses and [conceptual framework diagram](/blog/how-to-draw-a-conceptual-framework-diagram), including controls.
2. **Write a conceptual definition** for each, citing the literature.
3. **Choose an indicator** that matches the definition and your design.
4. **Find an instrument** or data source, and check its evidence and license.
5. **State the scoring,** unit or categories, and the level of measurement.
6. **Check alignment:** does each hypothesis now name measurable variables? Does the planned test suit their levels?
7. **Pilot** the instruments and procedures with a few participants or records.
8. **Fill in the table** and report your sample's reliability in the results.

## Common mistakes

- **Defining a variable with a synonym** ("stress is pressure") instead of a measurement.
- **A conceptual definition that the measure does not match,** for example a three-part concept measured with one subscale.
- **Unclear direction:** not saying whether higher scores mean more or less of the construct.
- **Treating an ordinal single item as ratio** without comment.
- **Inventing a questionnaire** when a validated one exists, or adapting one without piloting.
- **Citing validity evidence from a very different population** and not checking it in yours.
- **Using a licensed instrument without permission.**
- **Names that drift** between the framework, the questionnaire and the results.

## FAQ

### What is an operational definition of a variable?

An operational definition states exactly how a variable will be observed or measured in your study: the indicator, the instrument or procedure, the scoring, and the unit or categories. For example, "perceived stress, measured as the total score (0 to 40) on the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale". It lets another researcher repeat your measurement.

### What is the difference between a conceptual and an operational definition?

A conceptual definition says what a concept means, usually citing the literature or a theory. An operational definition says how you will measure that concept in this study. "Self-esteem is a person's overall evaluation of their own worth" is conceptual; "the total score on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale" is operational. A thesis needs both, and they must match.

### What are the four levels of measurement?

Nominal (categories with no order, such as blood type), ordinal (ordered categories, such as education level), interval (equal steps with no true zero, such as temperature in Celsius) and ratio (equal steps with a true zero, such as income or reaction time). The level decides which statistics and tests you can use.

### Where do operational definitions go in a thesis?

In the methodology chapter, usually in a section called Variables, Measures or Instruments, often as a table. Some programs also ask for a list of operational definitions of key terms in the first chapter. Either way, the measure for each variable must match the hypothesis and the conceptual framework.

### Can a qualitative study have operational definitions?

Qualitative studies do not usually measure variables, but they still need to say how a concept will be recognized in the data: what counts as an instance of "trust" or "resilience" in an interview, for example. That is often set out in a codebook with a definition and inclusion and exclusion rules for each code.
