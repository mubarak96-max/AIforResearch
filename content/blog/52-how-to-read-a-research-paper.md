# How to read a research paper quickly and critically

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

Here is how to read a research paper without wasting time: don't read it front to back. Read in up to three passes: a five-to-ten-minute skim of the title, abstract, introduction, headings and conclusions; an hour-long read of the figures and results if the paper matters; and a slow, critical rebuild of the work only for the few papers your project rests on.

This guide is for students and researchers who need to get through a reading list without losing the critical thinking. It is part of our series on literature reviews; the pillar guide, [how to write a literature review](/blog/how-to-write-a-literature-review), shows how your reading turns into a review.

## What is the three-pass method for reading a paper?

**The three-pass method is a way to read a research paper in up to three passes of increasing depth, each with a specific goal, instead of reading it once from start to finish.** It was set out by S. Keshav in a two-page article, "How to Read a Paper," in *ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review* in 2007 [1]. Each pass builds on the last, and you can stop after any of them.

| Pass | Time (Keshav's estimate) | What you read | What you can do after it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First | About 5 to 10 minutes | Title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusions, glance at references | Say what kind of paper it is and decide whether to go on |
| Second | Up to an hour | The whole paper with care, but skipping details such as proofs; figures and graphs closely | Summarize the main thrust, with supporting evidence, to someone else |
| Third | About 4 to 5 hours for beginners, about 1 hour for experienced readers | Everything, challenging every assumption | Rebuild the paper's structure from memory and name its strong and weak points |

All times and goals in the table are from Keshav's article [1].

### The first pass

The first pass is a quick scan to get a bird's-eye view. Keshav's steps [1]:

1. Carefully read the title, abstract and introduction.
2. Read the section and subsection headings, and ignore everything else.
3. Read the conclusions.
4. Glance over the references, ticking off the ones you've already read.

At the end you should be able to answer the **five Cs**: **Category** (what type of paper is this?), **Context** (which other papers and theories is it related to?), **Correctness** (do the assumptions appear valid?), **Contributions** (what are the main contributions?) and **Clarity** (is it well written?) [1]. If the paper isn't relevant, or the assumptions look wrong, stop here. The first pass is enough for papers outside your area that may become relevant someday.

### The second pass

Read with care but skip details such as proofs, and jot down key points in the margins [1]. Two specific instructions from the article:

- Look closely at the figures, diagrams and graphs. Are the axes labeled? Are results shown with error bars, so you can judge whether differences are meaningful?
- Mark relevant references you haven't read, for later.

If you still don't understand the paper after the second pass, Keshav gives three options: set it aside, come back after reading background material, or go on to the third pass [1].

### The third pass

The third pass is an attempt to virtually re-implement the paper: make the same assumptions as the authors and re-create the work, then compare your version with theirs [1]. That comparison exposes the paper's innovations and its hidden failings and assumptions. You should finish able to point to implicit assumptions, missing citations to relevant work and potential problems with the experimental or analytical methods [1]. For an empirical study outside computer science, "re-create" means: could you rerun this study from the methods section, and would you analyze the data the same way?

## What order should you read a research paper in?

Abstract, introduction, headings and conclusions first; figures and results next; methods last and in detail only when you rely on the paper. The order follows how much each section tells you per minute.

| Order | Section | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title and abstract | The question, the design, the headline result |
| 2 | Introduction (last paragraph especially) | The gap the authors claim and their exact aim |
| 3 | Headings | How the argument is built |
| 4 | Conclusions | What the authors claim, in their own words |
| 5 | Figures and tables | Whether the data show what the abstract says |
| 6 | Results | Effect sizes, confidence intervals, which outcomes were primary |
| 7 | Discussion and limitations | What the authors admit; whether the conclusions go further than the results |
| 8 | Methods | Sample, measures, comparison, analysis: the basis for trusting all of the above |

Steps 1 to 4 are the first pass. In the health sciences, Greenhalgh's long-running "How to read a paper" series makes a similar point: first decide what the paper is about and whether its design fits the question, before you invest in the detail [2].

## How do you critically analyze a research article?

Ask three questions in order: are the results valid, what are the results, and do they apply to your question? Critical appraisal checklists such as those from CASP are organized around questions like these, with separate versions for trials, cohort studies, qualitative studies, systematic reviews and other designs [3]. <!-- VERIFY: CASP checklists' three-section structure (validity, results, local applicability) in the current checklist PDFs -->

**Critical appraisal is the systematic assessment of a study's validity, results and relevance before you rely on it.**

Questions that work for most empirical papers:

| Question | What a weak answer looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Does the design fit the question? | A cross-sectional survey used to claim cause and effect |
| How were participants or data chosen? | Volunteers only; a single site; no response rate given |
| Was there a fair comparison? | No control group, or groups that differed at the start |
| Were outcomes measured well? | Self-report only, for an outcome that could be measured directly |
| Was the main outcome set in advance? | Many outcomes tested, only the significant ones reported |
| How big and precise is the effect? | A p-value with no effect size or confidence interval |
| Who dropped out? | High or uneven attrition, not addressed |
| Do the conclusions match the results? | The abstract claims more than the results table shows |
| Who funded it? | Undisclosed or conflicting interests |

Then place the study on the [levels of evidence](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) for your type of question. A well-run cohort study can be more useful than a poorly run trial.

## How to read a research paper quickly for a literature review

Use the first pass as a filter on everything, and save the deeper passes for the papers that earn them. *Example (invented numbers):* a reading list of 40 papers.

| Pass | Papers | Time each | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First pass | 40 | 10 minutes | about 7 hours |
| Second pass | 15 | 1 hour | 15 hours |
| Third pass | 3 | 4 hours | 12 hours |
| **Total** | | | **about 34 hours** |

Reading all 40 in full at even two hours each would take 80 hours. The three-pass budget gets you a defensible understanding of the field in less than half the time, with deep knowledge of the three papers that matter most. You don't read paper after paper in full; you read each one only as deeply as it deserves.

Keshav adds a method for surveying a new field: find three to five recent papers, do one pass on each and read their related work sections; then look for shared citations and repeated author names, which point to the key papers and researchers [1]. That is backward snowballing in practice; see [citation snowballing](/blog/citation-snowballing).

## How do you take notes that turn into writing?

Write the same short note for every paper, with page numbers, and add one line on where it fits in your work. Notes that only describe a paper have to be rewritten later; notes that compare it are halfway to a paragraph.

After the second pass, fill:

```
Five Cs:    category / context / correctness / contributions / clarity
Claim:      the main finding, with the number and page
Weakness:   the one thing that most limits it (p. 11)
Compare:    agrees with Study X, conflicts with Study Y because...
Use:        which section of my review, and which theme
```

The "Compare" line is the seed of synthesis. When you have 20 of them, move them into a synthesis matrix, as shown in [how to write a literature review](/blog/how-to-write-a-literature-review). For where to keep notes so they stay with the PDF, see [how to organize research papers](/blog/how-to-organize-research-papers).

## FAQ

### What order should you read a research paper in?

Not front to back. Read the title, abstract and introduction, then the section headings, then the conclusions, then glance at the references. That is the first pass in S. Keshav's three-pass method and takes five to ten minutes. If the paper is worth more time, read the figures and tables next, then the results and discussion, and the methods last, in detail only for papers you rely on.

### How long does it take to read a research paper?

It depends on how deeply you need it. Keshav's method gives a guide: about five to ten minutes for a first pass that tells you what the paper is and whether to continue, up to an hour for a second pass that lets you summarize it with evidence, and four to five hours for a beginner's third pass that rebuilds the work in detail. Most papers only need the first pass.

### How do you read a research paper quickly?

Read selectively. Use the first pass on every paper: title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusions and references, in five to ten minutes. Decide whether to go further. Give the second pass only to papers you will cite, and the third pass only to the few your work depends on. Keep a fixed note template so you don't reread papers to remember them.

### How do you critically analyze a research article?

Ask three questions: are the results valid, what are the results, and do they apply to your question? Check whether the design fits the question, how participants were chosen, whether outcomes were measured well, whether the analysis matches the plan, how big and precise the effect is, and whether the conclusions go beyond the data. Checklists such as CASP give study-specific questions.

### Can AI read a research paper for me?

AI can help you read faster by answering specific questions about a PDF with page numbers, finding where the methods describe something, or explaining a term. It cannot judge the paper for you, and it tends to state findings more broadly than the authors did. Do the first pass yourself, use AI to locate details, and check every answer on the cited page.
