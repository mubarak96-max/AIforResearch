# How to find sources for a research paper: 7 places to look and how to check them

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

To find sources for a research paper, turn your topic into two or three search concepts, search your library's databases and a scholarly search engine, then follow the reference lists and citing papers of the best results. Keep peer-reviewed articles, reviews, academic books and official reports. Before you cite anything, open it, confirm it exists and is not retracted, and read the part you rely on.

This guide is for students writing an essay, a research paper or a thesis chapter, and for anyone who needs citable evidence fast. It is part of our series on finding and checking sources; the pillar guide on [levels of evidence and study types](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) explains which kinds of study to trust first.

## What counts as a credible source for a research paper?

**A credible source is one whose author, publisher and method you can identify and check, and that other experts have reviewed or can verify.** For most research papers that means peer-reviewed journal articles, review articles, academic books, and reports from government agencies or established research organizations.

**A primary source reports original research or data; a secondary source summarizes or interprets other people's research.** Cite primary studies for specific findings and good reviews for the state of a field.

| Source type | Usually citable? | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peer-reviewed journal article | Yes | Retractions, small samples, predatory journals |
| Systematic review or meta-analysis | Yes, strong for "what research shows" | Search date (is it out of date?) |
| Academic book or chapter | Yes | Edition and year |
| Government or agency report | Yes, for data and policy | Who funded it, how data were collected |
| Preprint (arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv) | With care, labeled as a preprint | Not peer reviewed; check for a published version |
| Thesis or dissertation | Sometimes | Lower review bar than a journal |
| News article, blog, press release | As a pointer only | Find and cite the study behind it |
| Wikipedia, AI chatbot answer | No | Use for background, then follow the references |

Two checks help. The CRAAP test from California State University, Chico asks about a source's Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy and Purpose [1]. Lateral reading is faster for unfamiliar sites: instead of studying the page, open new tabs and see what others say about the publisher and author. In a Stanford study, professional fact checkers who read laterally reached sounder judgments in less time than historians and students who stayed on the page [2].

## Where can I find credible sources for a research paper? 7 places

Use more than one of these. Each covers a different slice of the literature.

| Place | Best for | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Library databases | Peer-reviewed articles in your subject, full text | Free through your university |
| 2. Google Scholar | Broad first search across fields | Free; full text via library links |
| 3. PubMed | Health, medicine, life sciences | Free |
| 4. Preprint servers (arXiv and others) | The newest work in physics, maths, computer science, biology | Free |
| 5. AI research search | A fast first map of a question, with citations | Free tiers vary |
| 6. Reference lists | Older, foundational work | Free |
| 7. Citation trails | Newer work that built on a key paper | Free indexes exist |

### 1. Your library's databases

Your library pays for subject databases (for example PsycINFO, ERIC, Scopus or Web of Science, depending on your institution) and links them to full text. Start from the library's website so the full-text links work. Ask a subject librarian which two databases matter most for your topic; it is a five-minute conversation that saves hours.

### 2. Google Scholar

Google Scholar searches across disciplines and shows a "Cited by" count under each result. Under Settings, then Library links, add your university so full-text links appear next to results; off campus, Scholar remembers your subscriptions for 30 days after you visit on campus [3]. Results include theses, preprints and non-peer-reviewed documents, so judge each one.

### 3. PubMed

For any health or biomedical topic, search PubMed. It indexes MEDLINE journals with MeSH subject terms and has filters for article type and free full text. Our guide on [how to search PubMed](/blog/how-to-search-pubmed) walks through a full search.

### 4. Preprint servers

arXiv, bioRxiv and medRxiv post papers before peer review. arXiv states plainly that its moderation "is not a peer-review process" [4]. Cite a preprint only when it is the best evidence available, label it as a preprint, and search for a published version before you submit.

### 5. AI research search

AI search tools turn a question into searches, read abstracts or full texts, and write a cited summary. They are good at the first map of a topic. They are not good at being right without checks (see below). For a comparison of tools, see [AI search engines for research](/blog/ai-search-engines-for-research).

### 6. Reference lists of good papers

When you find one strong, recent paper, read its introduction and reference list. Those references are the work the authors thought mattered. This is backward citation searching.

### 7. Citation trails

Forward citation searching finds newer papers that cite a key paper: follow-ups, replications and critiques. Our guide on [how to find who cited a paper](/blog/how-to-find-who-cited-a-paper) shows how in each tool, and [citation snowballing](/blog/citation-snowballing) turns both directions into a repeatable method.

## How to find sources for a research paper, step by step

Here is the best way to find sources for a research paper when you have a topic and a deadline.

1. **Write your question in one sentence.** "Does remote work increase burnout among office employees?"
2. **Split it into two or three concepts** and list synonyms for each: remote work (telework, work from home, telecommuting); burnout (emotional exhaustion, job stress); office employees (knowledge workers, white-collar).
3. **Build one search string.** Join synonyms with OR in brackets and concepts with AND: `("remote work" OR telework OR "work from home") AND (burnout OR "emotional exhaustion")`.
4. **Run it in two places**: your library's main database and Google Scholar (or PubMed for health topics). Limit to the last 5 to 10 years unless the topic is historical.
5. **Look for a review first.** Add "review" or "meta-analysis" to the search. A recent review gives you the field's map and a reference list.
6. **Screen by title, then abstract.** Keep papers that answer your question for your population. Discard the rest without guilt.
7. **Follow trails from your two or three best papers**: their reference lists (backward) and the papers that cite them (forward).
8. **Check every keeper** with the evaluation checklist below.
9. **Save each source with its full citation** in a reference manager as you go.

*Worked example (invented counts, for illustration).* The remote-work search above returns 1,240 results in a library database. Limiting to 2019 onward and peer-reviewed leaves 610. Scanning the first 100 titles gives 22 candidates; abstracts cut that to 9. One is a 2024 systematic review of 31 studies. Its reference list adds 4 relevant studies, and the papers citing it add 2 more from 2025. You finish with 15 sources: 1 review, 12 primary studies and 2 recent follow-ups, from 3 hours of work.

## Can you use AI to find sources, and are they real?

You can use AI to find sources, but only tools that search a real index of papers, and only if you verify every result. General chatbots answer from patterns in their training data and can produce references that look right and don't exist.

The numbers are not small. Walters and Wilder asked ChatGPT to write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references: 55% of GPT-3.5's citations and 18% of GPT-4's were fabricated, and many of the real ones had errors [5]. Newer tools that search the web or a scholarly index do better, because each citation points to a record you can open. That is a reason to check, not a reason to skip checking.

**An AI source finder is a tool that searches scholarly indexes for papers relevant to a question and links each result to its record.** Use it to find candidates; you still choose and read them.

### How do you check that a source is real?

Run four checks on any source, and on every source an AI tool suggests:

1. **It exists.** The DOI resolves at doi.org, or the title appears in a scholarly index with the same authors, year and journal.
2. **It says what you cite.** Open the abstract or full text and find the sentence or table you rely on. If you can't, don't cite it.
3. **It still stands.** Search the title with "retraction" and check the publisher's page for notices.
4. **It is what it claims to be.** A preprint, conference abstract or editorial is not the same as a peer-reviewed study. Label it correctly.

Our guide on [how to check a claim against the research](/blog/how-to-check-a-claim-against-the-research) covers step 2 in depth.

## How many sources should a research paper have?

There is no universal number. Use your assignment brief or the target journal's guidance first. If neither says, count claims rather than chasing a total.

1. List every statement in your draft that is a fact from outside your own work.
2. Give each one at least one source; give your central claims two independent studies or a review.
3. Remove duplicates where one source supports several statements.

*Example (invented).* A 3,000-word paper with 6 paragraphs of background, each making 3 outside claims, has 18 claims. Some sources support two claims, so 10 to 14 distinct sources is a sensible result for that draft. A literature review covers far more ground, so it needs far more.

**Rule:** quality beats count. Ten well-chosen peer-reviewed sources beat thirty loosely related ones, and markers notice padding.

## How should you save and organize sources?

Save each source the moment you decide to keep it, with its DOI, a PDF if you have legal access, and one line on why it matters. A reference manager (Zotero, Mendeley or similar) formats citations later in any style. Our guide on [how to organize research papers](/blog/how-to-organize-research-papers) covers folders, file names and notes.

## FAQ

### Where can I find credible sources for a research paper?

Start with your library's databases and a scholarly search engine such as Google Scholar, then a subject database: PubMed for health, arXiv for physics and computer science preprints, ERIC for education. Add the reference lists of the best papers you find and the newer papers that cite them. Peer-reviewed articles, review articles, academic books and official reports are the usual credible sources.

### How many sources should a research paper have?

There is no fixed number. Your assignment brief or the journal's guidance sets it, so check that first. Otherwise, count the claims that need support: every factual statement that isn't common knowledge or your own result needs a citation, and key claims are stronger with two independent studies. A short paper with few claims needs fewer sources than a review.

### Can I use AI to find sources?

Yes, if the tool searches a real index of papers and links each result to its record. Plain chatbots can invent references: one 2023 study found 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Whatever tool you use, open each source, check that its DOI or record exists, and read the part you cite before it goes in your paper.

### Is Google Scholar a credible source?

Google Scholar is a search engine, not a source. It finds journal articles, theses, books, preprints and some non-peer-reviewed documents, so the results vary in quality. Judge each result on its own: who published it, whether it was peer reviewed, and whether it has been retracted. Use your library's link in Scholar settings to reach full texts your university pays for.

### Can I cite Wikipedia in a research paper?

Most instructors and journals don't accept Wikipedia as a cited source, because anyone can edit it and it is a summary, not original research. It is still a useful starting point: read the article for background and terms, then follow its references to the books and papers behind it, read those, and cite them instead.
