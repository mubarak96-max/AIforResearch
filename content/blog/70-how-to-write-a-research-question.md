# How to write a research question that you can actually answer

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

To write a research question, start from a broad topic, find a gap in what is already known, narrow it to one population, one variable or phenomenon and one outcome, then test it against FINER (feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant). Frame it with PICO for effect questions, PCC for mapping questions, or SPIDER for qualitative ones.

This guide is for students and early-career researchers who need a question for a thesis, a dissertation, a proposal or a review. It is the hub of our series on planning a study, so it links down to the hypothesis, methodology, framework and outline guides that come next.

## What is a research question?

**A research question is a single, answerable question that defines what your study will find out, about whom, and how you will know when you have an answer.** Everything else in a thesis follows from it: the literature you read, the design, the sample, the analysis and the structure of your chapters.

A topic is not a question. "Social media and teenagers" is a topic. "Is daily time on short-video apps associated with sleep duration in 13 to 15 year olds in urban secondary schools?" is a question. The second version tells you who to sample, what to measure and which analysis to run.

### What are the 3 types of research questions?

Most questions fall into one of three types, and the type decides your design.

| Type | It asks | Example (illustrative) | Typical design |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Descriptive | What exists, how much, how often | What proportion of ICU nurses in public hospitals report burnout? | Survey, audit, content analysis |
| Comparative or relational | How groups differ, how variables move together | Is remote work frequency associated with job satisfaction among software developers? | Cross-sectional, correlational, comparative |
| Causal | Whether one thing changes another | Does a 10-week flipped classroom raise exam scores in first-year statistics? | Experiment, quasi-experiment |

Qualitative studies add a fourth kind in practice: exploratory questions that ask how people experience or make sense of something ("How do first-generation PhD students describe their relationship with their supervisor?"). These do not predict an outcome, which is why they rarely come with a hypothesis.

## How to write a research question in four steps

Use this procedure. It takes most people a few sessions, not one.

1. **Choose a topic you can live with.** Pick an area you can read about for a year and where you can get data: access to a hospital, a school, a company dataset, a lab instrument or a public archive.
2. **Read to find the gap.** Read recent reviews and the most cited papers. Note what is unknown, contested or untested in your setting. A gap is usually one of: an unstudied group, an untested method, conflicting results, or an old finding in a new context.
3. **Narrow to one sentence.** Name the population, the main variable or phenomenon, and the outcome or context. Cut words such as "impact of", "role of" and "factors affecting" unless you can say which factors and how you will measure them.
4. **Test and frame it.** Run it through FINER (below), then write it in a framework (PICO, PCC or SPIDER) to check that every part is specific. Show it to your supervisor before you write the proposal.

### Worked example: from topic to question (illustrative)

- **Topic:** mobile money and small businesses.
- **Gap found:** most studies cover Kenya and urban traders; few cover women-owned rural businesses in Tanzania.
- **Draft question:** "What is the impact of mobile money on women entrepreneurs?"
- **Problems:** "impact" is vague, no setting, no outcome.
- **Final question:** "Among women-owned rural retail businesses in two regions of Tanzania, is regular use of mobile money associated with higher monthly revenue after 12 months?"

## What are the FINER criteria?

**The FINER criteria are a five-point test of a research question: feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant.** They come from Hulley and colleagues' textbook *Designing Clinical Research* [1], and are now used well beyond medicine.

| Criterion | Ask yourself | A fail looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Feasible** | Can I get enough participants or data, with the skills, time and money I have, in a scope I can manage? | A national survey for a 12-month master's |
| **Interesting** | Does it interest me, my supervisor and my field? | A question nobody would read the answer to |
| **Novel** | Does it confirm, refute or extend earlier findings? | Repeating a well-settled result in the same setting |
| **Ethical** | Would an ethics committee approve it? | Withholding a known effective treatment |
| **Relevant** | Will the answer matter for knowledge, practice or policy? | A result that changes nothing |

Feasibility kills more student projects than any other criterion. Before you commit, write down three numbers: how many participants or records you need, how many you can realistically reach, and how many months you have for data collection. If the second number is smaller than the first, narrow the population or change the design. Our [statistical power and sample size](/blog/statistical-power-and-sample-size) guide shows how to get the first number.

## How do you write a PICO question?

**PICO is a framework for questions about the effect of an intervention or exposure: population, intervention, comparison, outcome.** It was set out by Richardson and colleagues in 1995 as the anatomy of a "well-built clinical question" [2]. Adding T for time gives PICOT [3].

### PICO worked example 1: health (illustrative)

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Adults aged 65 and over living at home with one or more falls in the past year |
| Intervention | A 12-week home-based balance and strength program |
| Comparison | Usual care |
| Outcome | Number of falls over the following 12 months |
| Time (PICOT) | 12-month follow-up |

**Question:** "In adults aged 65 and over living at home who fell in the past year, does a 12-week home-based balance and strength program, compared with usual care, reduce the number of falls over 12 months?"

### PICO worked example 2: education (illustrative)

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Population | First-year undergraduate statistics students at one university |
| Intervention | A flipped classroom (video lectures before class, problem solving in class) |
| Comparison | Traditional lectures |
| Outcome | Final exam score out of 100 |

**Question:** "Do first-year statistics students taught with a flipped classroom score higher on the final exam than students taught with traditional lectures?"

PICO also works outside health whenever you compare an intervention. In engineering: "In reinforced concrete beams (P), does adding 1% steel fiber (I), compared with no fiber (C), increase flexural strength at 28 days (O)?"

## What is the PCC framework?

**PCC (population, concept, context) is the framework JBI recommends for scoping review questions, where the aim is to map evidence rather than test an effect** [4]. It drops the comparison and outcome because a scoping review asks what has been studied, not what works.

### PCC worked example: social science (illustrative)

| Element | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Refugee children aged 5 to 12 |
| Concept | School-based mental health support programs |
| Context | High-income host countries |

**Question:** "What school-based mental health support programs for refugee children aged 5 to 12 have been described in high-income host countries?"

If you are choosing between review types, our [scoping review vs systematic review](/blog/scoping-review-vs-systematic-review) guide has a decision table.

## When should you use SPIDER instead?

**SPIDER (sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, research type) is a framework for qualitative and mixed-methods questions**, proposed by Cooke, Smith and Booth in 2012 as an alternative to PICO for qualitative evidence searches [5].

Example (illustrative), business: Sample, frontline retail managers; Phenomenon of interest, introducing self-checkout; Design, interviews; Evaluation, experiences and perceived effects on staff roles; Research type, qualitative. **Question:** "How do frontline retail managers experience the introduction of self-checkout, and how do they describe its effects on staff roles?"

| Framework | Best for | Elements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PICO / PICOT | Effect of an intervention or exposure | Population, intervention, comparison, outcome (time) |
| PCC | Scoping and mapping reviews | Population, concept, context |
| SPIDER | Qualitative and mixed-methods questions | Sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, research type |

## Research question examples: five weak questions fixed

These are examples, written for this guide. Each shows the fault and the fix.

| Field | Weak question | Fault | Better question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Health | How does diet affect diabetes? | Too broad; no population, measure or time | In adults with type 2 diabetes in primary care, is following a Mediterranean diet for 6 months associated with lower HbA1c than usual dietary advice? |
| Education | Is homework good? | Value judgment, not measurable | Is weekly time spent on math homework associated with end-of-year math scores in grade 8 students in public schools? |
| Business | Why do employees leave? | No population; "why" is unbounded | What reasons do software developers who left mid-size firms in the past two years give for leaving, and how do they rank them? |
| Engineering | Can AI improve traffic? | Vague intervention and outcome | Does an adaptive signal-timing algorithm reduce average intersection delay at peak hours compared with fixed timing, in a simulation of a 12-junction city corridor? |
| Social science | What do people think about climate change? | Yes or no plus no population | How do smallholder farmers in northern Ghana describe changes in rainfall, and what adaptations do they report making? |

The pattern is the same each time: name the people, name the measure or phenomenon, name the setting, and remove words that judge before you have data.

## How long should a research question be?

One sentence, usually 15 to 35 words. If yours runs longer, check for two questions joined by "and". Keep one as the main question and turn the others into sub-questions. A thesis commonly has one main question and two to four sub-questions, each answered in a results section or chapter.

## FAQ

### What makes a good research question?

A good research question is focused, answerable with data you can get, and worth answering. It names who or what you will study, what you will look at, and what result would count as an answer. The FINER test sums this up: it should be feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant. If you cannot picture the table or theme list that would answer it, it is not ready.

### What are the 3 types of research questions?

Most research questions are descriptive, comparative or relational, or causal. A descriptive question asks what exists or how much (what share of nurses report burnout). A comparative or relational question asks how groups differ or how variables move together. A causal question asks whether one thing changes another, which usually needs an experiment or a strong quasi-experimental design. The type you choose decides your design.

### How long should a research question be?

One sentence, usually 15 to 35 words. It must be long enough to name the population, the main variable or phenomenon, and the outcome or context, and short enough to read in one breath. If it needs two sentences or several 'and' clauses, you probably have two questions. Split them into a main question and sub-questions.

### What is the difference between PICO and PCC?

PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) fits questions about the effect of a treatment or exposure, so it suits trials and systematic reviews of effectiveness. PCC (population, concept, context) comes from JBI guidance for scoping reviews, where the aim is to map what is known rather than measure an effect. Use PICO when you compare; use PCC when you explore or map.

### Can AI write my research question?

AI can suggest candidate questions from a topic and show which papers already address them, which saves time at the start. It cannot know your supervisor's expectations, your access to data or your deadline, so treat its output as a shortlist. Test each candidate against FINER yourself, check the papers it cites, and rewrite the one you keep in your own words.
