# What is a thesis statement? Definition, length, formula and examples

*Last updated: 14 November 2026*

A thesis statement is one or two sentences, usually at the end of the introduction, that state the main claim of your essay and the main reason for it. It answers the assignment's question with a position a reader could dispute, and it tells the reader what every body paragraph will support. In most school and college essays it is one sentence of about 20 to 40 words.

This is the hub of our series on essays, theses and sources. It covers the definition, the parts, the length, a formula and strong and weak examples for eight essay types. If you want to use AI to brainstorm or test a thesis, read [how to write a thesis statement with AI](/blog/how-to-write-a-thesis-statement-with-ai), which has 15 more examples and prompts.

## What is a thesis statement?

**A thesis statement is the sentence that tells the reader what your paper argues or explains, and why.** Purdue OWL says it should cover only what you will discuss in the paper and be supported with specific evidence [1]. UNC's Writing Center puts it as "an interpretation of a question or subject, not the subject itself" [2].

Three things are not a thesis:

- **A topic.** "Remote work" is a topic.
- **A fact.** "Many companies adopted remote work in 2020" is a fact. No one argues with it.
- **An announcement.** "This essay will discuss remote work" says what the essay is about, not what it claims.

A thesis turns the topic into a claim: "Hybrid schedules suit software teams better than fully remote ones, because scheduled office days protect the informal mentoring that junior developers need" (illustrative).

## Where does the thesis statement go?

**Put the thesis statement at the end of your introduction, usually as its last sentence.** Purdue OWL recommends the end of the first paragraph for most papers [1]. The sentences before it set up the problem; the sentence after it is the first body paragraph.

| Paper | Where the thesis goes |
| --- | --- |
| Five-paragraph or short essay (500 to 1,200 words) | Last sentence of paragraph 1 |
| College essay (1,500 to 3,000 words) | End of the introduction, which may be one or two paragraphs |
| Research paper (10+ pages) | End of the introduction section, sometimes restated at the start of the discussion |
| Thesis or dissertation | The central argument appears in the abstract, at the end of chapter 1, and again in the conclusion |

Restate the thesis in the conclusion in new words, adding what the body has shown. Do not paste it in unchanged.

## What are the parts of a thesis statement?

**A working thesis has three parts: a topic, a claim about it, and a reason or roadmap.** Booth, Colomb and Williams describe an argument as a claim backed by reasons, which rest on evidence [3]. Your thesis carries the first two; the body supplies the evidence.

| Part | What it does | Example (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topic | Names the narrow subject | Four-day school weeks in rural districts |
| Claim | Takes a position on it | ...save less money than districts expect |
| Reason or roadmap | Says why, or previews the points | ...because transport and staffing costs fall less than the calendar suggests |

Some theses add a fourth part, a **concession**: "Although four-day weeks help with teacher recruitment, they save less money than districts expect, because..." The concession shows you know the other side, which Graff and Birkenstein treat as the core move of academic writing: state what "they say", then what "I say" [4].

## How long should a thesis statement be?

**One sentence of about 20 to 40 words for most essays; two sentences for a longer research paper.** There is no official word count in any style guide. The length follows from the job: long enough for a claim and a reason, short enough to read in one breath.

| Paper length | Thesis length | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 3 pages | 1 sentence, 20 to 30 words | Claim and one reason |
| 4 to 10 pages | 1 sentence, 25 to 40 words | Claim, reason, sometimes a concession |
| 10 to 25 pages | 1 or 2 sentences, up to about 60 words | Second sentence previews the structure |
| Thesis or dissertation | A short paragraph stating the central argument | Supported by research questions in chapter 1 |

Too long: several "and"s joining separate claims, or five or more points. Too short: no "because", or it fits on a bumper sticker ("Social media is bad").

A thesis statement is different from a thesis, the long document. If you need page or word counts for a master's thesis or PhD dissertation, see [how long is a thesis or dissertation](/blog/how-long-is-a-thesis).

## A thesis statement formula

**Use this formula for a first draft: [Specific topic] + [claim a reader could dispute] + because [main reason].** Then rewrite it so it reads naturally.

Three variations:

1. **With a concession:** Although [opposing view], [claim] because [reason].
2. **With a roadmap:** [Claim], as shown by [point 1], [point 2] and [point 3].
3. **For analysis:** Through [technique or feature], [author or text] shows [interpretation].
## How to write a thesis statement in five steps

**Turn the prompt into a question, answer it, test the answer against your evidence, then sharpen it.**

1. **Find the question in the prompt.** "Discuss the effects of tourism on Venice" becomes "Has mass tourism done more harm than good to Venice?"
2. **Write a working answer** before you finish researching. It will change, but it gives your reading a direction.
3. **Gather evidence on both sides.** Note two or three strong sources for and against. Check that each source is trustworthy: see [how to evaluate source credibility](/blog/how-to-evaluate-source-credibility), and know whether you are reading [primary or secondary sources](/blog/primary-vs-secondary-sources).
4. **Revise the claim to match the evidence.** If your sources support a narrower claim, narrow the sentence. If they disagree, add a concession or soften the verb.
5. **Test it** with the checklist at the end of this post, and revise again after the draft. Papers change as you write [1].

### Worked example (illustrative)

- **Prompt:** Evaluate a city's response to mass tourism.
- **Working thesis:** "Tourism is bad for Venice."
- **Problem:** vague ("bad"), too broad, no reason.
- **After reading:** the strongest evidence concerns housing loss and day-trippers.
- **Final thesis:** "Venice's day-tripper fee treats the symptom rather than the cause, because the city's population loss is driven by short-term rentals, which the fee does not touch."

## Strong and weak thesis statement examples by essay type

Every example below is illustrative: it shows the form, not a checked finding. Build yours from your own sources.

### Argumentative essay

An argumentative thesis takes a side on a debatable question and gives a reason.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plastic pollution is a big problem. | A fact, no position | National governments should tax single-use plastic packaging at the producer level, because bans on consumer items like straws shift waste rather than reduce it. |
| Some people think college should be free and others do not. | Sits on the fence | Community college should be tuition-free for students under 25, because the cost is modest compared with the lifetime tax revenue from higher earnings. |

**Fix:** pick one side, name who should act, and add "because".

### Analytical essay

An analytical thesis breaks something into parts and says what they show.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The 2008 financial crisis had many causes. | True of everything | The 2008 crisis spread beyond the housing market mainly because banks held mortgage securities they could not value, which turned local losses into a freeze on lending. |
| This chart shows trends in energy use. | Describes, does not interpret | The chart shows that the drop in coal use since 2010 came mostly from natural gas, not renewables, which complicates claims about the source of emission cuts. |

**Fix:** replace "many causes" or "trends" with the one you will argue matters most.

### Expository essay

An expository thesis explains how something works or happened, without arguing for a side. It still needs a clear organizing idea.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| This essay is about how vaccines work. | An announcement | Vaccines protect people by training the immune system to recognize an antigen, produce antibodies and memory cells, and respond faster on later exposure. |
| There are many steps in the peer review process. | No steps named | Journal peer review moves a manuscript through editorial screening, review by two or three experts, a decision, and usually at least one round of revision. |

**Fix:** name the parts or stages, in the order your body paragraphs will cover them.

### Compare-and-contrast essay

A compare-and-contrast thesis says what the comparison reveals, not only that two things differ.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Online and in-person classes have similarities and differences. | True of any two things | Online classes match in-person classes for lecture content but fall behind on lab skills, because practical skills need hands-on feedback that video cannot give. |
| Frankenstein and Jurassic Park are both about science. | No point to the comparison | Both *Frankenstein* and *Jurassic Park* blame their disasters on scientists who refuse responsibility, but Shelley locates the failure in one man's pride while Crichton locates it in commercial pressure. |

**Fix:** finish the sentence "Comparing them shows that..."

### Persuasive essay

A persuasive thesis is close to an argumentative one but aims to move a specific audience to act. It often names the audience and the action.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Everyone should recycle more. | No audience, no specific action | Our school board should replace bottled water in vending machines with free refill stations, because the switch pays for itself within two years and cuts plastic waste on campus. |
| Voting is important. | A platitude | First-year students should register to vote at their college address, because local elections decide rents, transit and campus safety, the issues that affect them most. |

**Fix:** name who should do what, and give the reason that audience cares about.

### Research paper

A research paper thesis rests on sources and often uses cautious verbs, because the evidence is reported, not proven by you.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social media affects mental health. | Too broad, unclear direction | Among teenagers, the link between heavy social media use and low mood appears to work partly through lost sleep, which suggests that limiting night-time use may matter more than limiting total time. |
| Electric cars are better for the environment. | Unqualified; depends on context | Over their lifetime, electric cars produce lower emissions than gasoline cars in most regions, but the size of the gap depends on how the local grid generates electricity. |

**Fix:** add the population or context, and use "appears to", "is associated with" or "in most cases" when your sources do. Then cite every part: our [citation generator](/tools/citation-generator) formats each source in your style for free.

### Literary analysis

A literary thesis interprets the text and names the device or pattern that supports the reading.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *The Great Gatsby* is about the American Dream. | Plot summary, not interpretation | In *The Great Gatsby*, Fitzgerald uses Gatsby's parties to show that wealth can buy an audience but not belonging. |
| Shakespeare uses a lot of imagery in *Macbeth*. | Names a device, not its effect | In *Macbeth*, blood imagery shifts from a mark of honor in battle to a sign of guilt that cannot be washed off, tracing Macbeth's fall. |

**Fix:** answer "so what does the device do?" in the same sentence. For speeches and nonfiction, the equivalent is a rhetorical thesis: see [rhetorical analysis](/blog/rhetorical-analysis).

### Synthesis essay

A synthesis thesis takes a position that draws on several sources at once, rather than on one.

| Weak | Why it is weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The sources have different views on laptops in class. | Reports the sources, makes no claim | Instructors should allow laptops for note-taking but ban other uses during lectures, because the evidence against laptop notes is mixed while the evidence on distraction is consistent. |
| Source A says X and Source B says Y. | Summary, not synthesis | The case for later school start times rests more on sleep and attendance than on grades, where the evidence is thinner. |

**Fix:** write the claim that only someone who read all the sources could make. See [how to write a synthesis essay](/blog/synthesis-essay).

## Thesis statement vs research question vs hypothesis

**A research question asks, a hypothesis predicts, and a thesis statement argues.**

| | Research question | Hypothesis | Thesis statement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Form | A question | A testable prediction | A claim with a reason |
| Used in | Theses, dissertations, empirical studies, reviews | Quantitative studies | Essays, research papers, the central argument of a thesis |
| When written | Before data collection | Before data collection | Working version early; final version after the evidence |
| Example (illustrative) | Does a 10-week flipped classroom raise exam scores in first-year statistics? | Students in the flipped classroom will score higher than students in lectures. | The flipped classroom raised exam scores mainly for students with weaker prior math, which suggests it closes gaps more than it raises the ceiling. |

For more, see [how to write a research question](/blog/how-to-write-a-research-question) and [how to write a hypothesis](/blog/how-to-write-a-hypothesis).

## Thesis statement checklist

Run each draft through these nine questions.

1. Does it answer the question in the prompt?
2. Could a reasonable reader disagree with it (argumentative, persuasive, analytical)?
3. Is it specific: one narrow topic, a named group or text, a clear claim?
4. Does it give a reason, or preview the main points?
5. Can you support every part with evidence you have or can find in time?
6. Is it one sentence, or two for a long paper, and under about 40 words if it is one?
7. Does it avoid vague value words ("good", "bad", "important") and overclaims ("proves", "always")?
8. Does it pass "so what?": would anyone care about the answer? [2]
9. Does every body paragraph support some part of it, and have you revised it to match the finished draft?

## FAQ

### What is a thesis statement in simple words?

A thesis statement is the one or two sentences that say what your essay argues or explains. It answers the assignment's question with a position a reader could question, and it tells the reader what the rest of the paper will support. It usually comes at the end of the introduction. Every body paragraph should back up some part of it.

### How long should a thesis statement be?

Usually one sentence of about 20 to 40 words in a high school or college essay. A longer paper, such as a 15-page research paper, can use two sentences, where the second previews the structure. It needs room for a claim and a reason, but no more. If your thesis needs three sentences or a full paragraph, it is not focused yet.

### Where does the thesis statement go?

In most essays the thesis statement goes at the end of the introduction, often as the last sentence of the first paragraph. In a longer paper the introduction may run two or three paragraphs, and the thesis comes at the end of that opening section. Some fields and genres delay it, but in school and college writing, put it early unless your instructor says otherwise.

### What is the difference between a strong and a weak thesis statement?

A strong thesis takes a specific position that a reasonable reader could dispute and gives the main reason for it. A weak thesis states a fact, announces a topic, sits on the fence or is too broad to support in the space you have. The fix is almost always the same: narrow the subject, add a 'because' and choose verbs that commit to a claim.

### Is a thesis statement the same as a research question or hypothesis?

No. A research question asks what a study will find out. A hypothesis predicts the answer in a form you can test with data. A thesis statement states the position the paper argues. Essays lead with a thesis; empirical studies lead with a question and, for quantitative designs, a hypothesis, then state their main argument once the results are in.

### Can a thesis statement be a question?

No. A question tells the reader what you are thinking about, not what you think. You can open the introduction with a question to set up the problem, but the thesis itself must answer it with a claim. Turn 'Should schools ban phones?' into 'Schools should ban phones in class because...' and finish the sentence with your main reason.
