# How to find references for a claim you've already written

*Last updated: 12 November 2026*

To find references for a claim you've already written, split the sentence into single claims, turn each into a search of two or three key concepts, and search a scholarly index rather than asking a chatbot. Open the best result and find the passage that supports your sentence. If no paper supports the claim as worded, change the sentence, not the evidence.

This guide is for students and researchers who drafted first and now need citations: the "I wrote it, now I need a source" problem. It is part of our series on finding and checking sources; the pillar guide on [levels of evidence and study types](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) explains which papers carry the most weight.

## Why is adding references after writing risky?

Drafting before citing is normal. The risk is that you go looking for confirmation, and you find it. A search engine will return something for almost any sentence, and a paper on the right topic can still say something different from your claim.

**A quotation error is a citation where the cited source does not support, or contradicts, the statement it is attached to.** They are common even in published work. A meta-analysis of 28 studies of medical journal articles found quotation errors in 25.4% of citations, and major errors, where the source fails to support or contradicts the claim, in 11.9% [1].

The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors puts the responsibility plainly: authors must be able to confirm that each cited reference supports the statement it is cited for, and should verify references against the original sources [2]. Your instructor or examiner will expect the same.

So the goal is not "find a reference". It is "find out what the evidence says about my sentence, and cite it accurately".

## How to find references for a claim: 6 steps

Use this procedure for each sentence that needs a citation.

1. **Mark every claim that needs a source.** Highlight facts from outside your own work: numbers, findings, "studies show", causal statements. Skip common knowledge and your own argument.
2. **Split compound sentences.** "Screen time harms sleep and lowers grades in teenagers" is two claims, and each needs its own support.
3. **Turn each claim into a search** (next section).
4. **Search a scholarly index**, not a chatbot. Look for a review first, then primary studies.
5. **Read the passage that supports your claim** in the abstract, results or discussion. Note the page or section.
6. **Match or rewrite.** If the paper supports your wording, cite it. If it supports a narrower claim, narrow your sentence. If nothing supports it, rewrite or cut it.

## How do you turn a claim into a search query?

Pull out the concepts, drop the verbs and opinions, and add synonyms. Search engines match concepts; your sentence's rhetoric only gets in the way.

| Your sentence | Concepts | Search string |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Teenagers who use phones at night sleep less." | adolescents; screen use at bedtime; sleep duration | `(adolescen* OR teenager*) AND ("screen time" OR smartphone OR "media use") AND (bedtime OR night) AND ("sleep duration" OR sleep)` |
| "Mindfulness programs reduce anxiety in university students." | mindfulness intervention; anxiety; university students | `mindfulness AND anxiety AND ("university students" OR "college students")` then add `"meta-analysis"` |
| "Remote workers report more burnout." | remote work; burnout | `("remote work" OR telework OR "work from home") AND (burnout OR "emotional exhaustion")` |

Three rules make the search work:

- **Search the outcome you claimed.** If you wrote "lowers grades", search academic performance, not "learning".
- **Search for reviews first.** A systematic review or meta-analysis gives you the overall answer and its reference list in one source.
- **Search the opposite too.** Add terms like "no association" to see whether studies disagree. Our guide on [how to check a claim against the research](/blog/how-to-check-a-claim-against-the-research) covers this step and how to weigh conflicting studies.

The asterisk (`*`) is a truncation wildcard in most library databases and PubMed; Google Scholar doesn't support it, so type the word forms out there.

## Can AI find references for my essay?

AI can find candidate references quickly, but only tools that search a real index of papers are safe to use for this, and you still have to read what they find.

Chatbots that write without searching can produce references that look complete and don't exist. When Walters and Wilder checked 636 references from literature reviews written by ChatGPT, 55% of GPT-3.5's and 18% of GPT-4's were fabricated [3]. A reference that exists can still be wrong for your sentence, which is the more common and harder-to-spot problem.

**An AI reference finder is a tool that takes a claim or question, searches scholarly indexes and returns real papers that may support it, each linked to its record.** Use one this way:

1. Paste one claim, not a whole essay.
2. Open every suggested paper's record. Confirm the title, authors, year and DOI.
3. Read the abstract or the relevant section. Does it match your population, exposure and outcome?
4. Keep only papers that pass. Rewrite your sentence if they support something narrower.
5. Run your final reference list through a citation checker for retractions and errors.

If your course has rules on AI use, they usually cover search tools too; check them and disclose the use if asked.

## What should you do when no source supports your claim?

Change the sentence. A claim with no evidence is either common knowledge, your own argument, or wrong, and each has a fix.

| Situation | What it means | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common knowledge ("Water boils at 100°C at sea level") | No citation needed in most fields | Leave it, or ask your instructor where the line is |
| Your interpretation | It's your argument, not a finding | Signal it: "I argue that", "This suggests" |
| Evidence is narrower than your claim | Partly supported | Narrow it: add the population, setting or size |
| Evidence is mixed | Studies disagree | Say so: "Findings are mixed; X found..., Y found..." |
| No studies at all | Untested | "No study has tested..." (only after a real search), or cut it |
| Evidence says the opposite | Not supported | Delete or reverse the claim |

### Worked example: rewriting to match the evidence

*Example (invented papers and numbers, for illustration).* Your draft says: "Social media use causes depression in teenagers."

You search `(adolescen*) AND ("social media") AND (depress*) AND (review OR meta-analysis)`. The best result is a meta-analysis of 20 cross-sectional and cohort studies. It reports a small positive association (pooled r = 0.11) and says most studies can't establish direction.

The evidence supports a smaller, non-causal claim. Your new sentence: "Across 20 studies, teenagers who used social media more reported slightly more depressive symptoms, though the studies could not show which came first (Author, Year)." The reference is now accurate, and the sentence is stronger because a marker can check it.

## Should you cite a source you found cited in another paper?

Cite the original if you can read it. **A secondary citation is a reference to a work you know only through another source.** APA Style allows it with "as cited in", for example (Rabbitt, 1982, as cited in Lyon et al., 2014), and you list only the source you actually read [4]. Use it sparingly. Papers often misreport their sources, as the 25.4% quotation error rate above shows, so the original may not say what the second paper claims.

## FAQ

### Can AI find references for my essay?

Yes, if it searches a real index of papers and links every result to its record; a tool that only generates text can invent references. Paste one claim at a time, open each suggested paper, and confirm it says what your sentence says. If no paper supports the claim as worded, rewrite the sentence to match the evidence. You are responsible for every reference in your essay, whoever found it.

### How do I find a source for a statement?

Split the statement into single claims, pull out the two or three key concepts in each, and search those concepts with synonyms in a scholarly database or Google Scholar. Add 'review' to find a summary of the evidence. Open the best result, find the passage that supports your statement, and cite that paper. If you only find the statement in news or blogs, trace it to the study behind it.

### Is it wrong to add references after writing?

No. Many writers draft first and cite after. The risk is in how you do it: picking any paper that seems to fit, citing a source you haven't read, or keeping a sentence the evidence doesn't support. Read the passage you cite, let the evidence change your wording, and the order in which you wrote the paper doesn't matter.

### What if I can't find a source for my claim?

Treat that as information. Search once more with synonyms and a review filter. If nothing turns up, either the claim is common knowledge that needs no citation, it is your own argument (say so: 'I argue that'), or it is unsupported and should be narrowed, softened or cut. Never cite a paper that doesn't support the sentence just to fill the gap.

### Can I cite a source I found cited in another paper?

Read and cite the original if you can get it. If you can't, APA Style allows a secondary citation: name the original and add 'as cited in' the source you read, and list only the source you read in your references. Use this sparingly. Papers often summarize their sources inaccurately, so the original may not say what the second paper claims.
