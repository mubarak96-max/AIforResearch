# How to use et al. in citations

*Last updated: 14 November 2026*

Use "et al." (Latin for "and others") after the first author's surname to shorten a citation for a work with several authors: (Simmons et al., 2011). Write it with no period after "et" and a period after "al.". Each style sets its own threshold: APA and MLA use it for three or more authors, Chicago 18 for three or more in the text, and Vancouver and AMA mainly in the reference list once a work passes six authors [1][2][3].

This guide is part of our citation styles series; the pillar is [references in APA](/blog/apa-reference-list). For APA-only author rules, see [how to cite two or three authors in APA](/blog/how-to-cite-two-or-three-authors-apa).

## What does et al. mean?

**Et al. abbreviates the Latin et alii (masculine), et aliae (feminine) or et alia (neuter), all meaning "and others".** In citations it stands in for the coauthors you leave out.

- "Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011)" becomes "Simmons et al. (2011)".
- It always refers to people (authors or editors). For things, use "etc." ("tables, figures, etc.").
- The first author named is the first author printed on the paper, never the most famous one or the first alphabetically.

## How do you punctuate et al.?

**"et" is a whole word and takes no period; "al." is an abbreviation and always takes one.** That gives "et al." with a space and one period.

| Incorrect | Correct | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| et. al. | et al. | "et" is not abbreviated |
| et.al. | et al. | Needs a space |
| et al (APA, MLA, Chicago) | et al. | "al." needs its period (AMA is the exception) |
| (Simmons et al. 2011) in APA | (Simmons et al., 2011) | APA puts a comma before the year |
| (Simmons et al., 1360) in MLA | (Simmons et al. 1360) | MLA has no comma before the page |
| ...found by Simmons et al.. | ...found by Simmons et al. | One period ends both |
| Simmons et al. shows | Simmons et al. show | Plural: "and others" |

A comma before "et al." appears only when two or more names come first. In APA that happens only when you must write extra names to tell two works apart: (Kapoor, Bloom, Montez, et al., 2017). *Example (illustrative).* In MLA and Chicago reference entries, a comma separates the first author's reversed name from "et al.": "Wilkinson, Mark D., et al." [2][3].

## When do you use et al.? The thresholds by style

**Most author-date styles shorten in the text at three authors; numbered styles mainly shorten in the reference list, at more than six.** The table sets out the rules for the styles you will meet most.

| Style | In the text, et al. from | In the reference list |
| --- | --- | --- |
| APA 7 [1] | 3 or more authors, from the first citation | Never. List up to 20; for 21+, first 19, ". . .", last author |
| MLA 9 [2] | 3 or more authors | 3 or more: first author, then "et al." |
| Chicago 18 (2024) [3] | 3 or more authors (notes and author-date) | Up to 6: list all; 7 or more: first 3, then "et al." |
| Chicago 17 (2017) [4] | 4 or more authors | Up to 10: list all; 11 or more: first 7, then "et al." |
| Harvard (Cite Them Right) [5] | 4 or more authors; "*et al.*" in italics | Varies by institution; many list all authors |
| IEEE [6] | Numbers in brackets; "et al." only when you name authors in a sentence | More than 6: first author, then "*et al.*" |
| Vancouver (ICMJE, NLM) [7][8] | Numbers; "et al." only when you name authors | More than 6: first 6, then "et al." |
| AMA 11 [9] | Superscript numbers; "et al" (no period) for more than 2 named authors | More than 6: first 3, then "et al" |

Journals and universities often override these defaults. A journal using Vancouver may list three authors before "et al.", and a university Harvard guide may want every name. The journal's author guidelines or your course handbook wins over the manual.

## The same papers in seven styles

**Seeing one paper in every style makes the thresholds concrete.** The first example has three authors, so the styles disagree in the text. The second has 53 authors, so they disagree in the list.

### A three-author paper in the text

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011), *Psychological Science*, 22(11), 1359–1366, https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417632.

| Style | Parenthetical or numbered | Narrative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| APA 7 | (Simmons et al., 2011) | Simmons et al. (2011) |
| MLA 9 | (Simmons et al. 1360) | Simmons et al. argue... (1360) |
| Chicago 18, author-date | (Simmons et al. 2011, 1360) | Simmons et al. (2011, 1360) |
| Chicago 17, author-date | (Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn 2011, 1360) | Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011) |
| Harvard (Cite Them Right) | (Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn, 2011) | Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) |
| IEEE | [3] | Simmons *et al.* [3] |
| Vancouver | (3) or superscript ³ | Simmons et al. (3) |
| AMA | superscript ³ | Simmons et al³ |

The page "1360" is illustrative, for a quotation from that page. The reference numbers assume it is the third source cited.

Harvard and Chicago 17 still write three names in full, because their thresholds are four. That is the most common reason the same paper looks different in two classes.

### A 53-author paper in the reference list

The FAIR Guiding Principles paper (Wilkinson et al., 2016) has 53 authors.

**APA 7** (first 19, ellipsis, last):

> Wilkinson, M. D., Dumontier, M., Aalbersberg, I. J., Appleton, G., Axton, M., Baak, A., Blomberg, N., Boiten, J.-W., da Silva Santos, L. B., Bourne, P. E., Bouwman, J., Brookes, A. J., Clark, T., Crosas, M., Dillo, I., Dumon, O., Edmunds, S., Evelo, C. T., Finkers, R., . . . Mons, B. (2016). The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship. *Scientific Data*, *3*, Article 160018. https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.18

**MLA 9** (first author, et al.):

> Wilkinson, Mark D., et al. "The FAIR Guiding Principles for Scientific Data Management and Stewardship." *Scientific Data*, vol. 3, 2016, https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.18.

**Chicago 18, bibliography** (first three, et al.):

> Wilkinson, Mark D., Michel Dumontier, IJsbrand Jan Aalbersberg, et al. "The FAIR Guiding Principles for Scientific Data Management and Stewardship." *Scientific Data* 3 (2016): 160018. https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.18.

**IEEE** (first author, *et al.*):

> M. D. Wilkinson *et al.*, "The FAIR guiding principles for scientific data management and stewardship," *Sci. Data*, vol. 3, 2016, Art. no. 160018, doi: 10.1038/sdata.2016.18.

**Vancouver** (first six, et al.):

> Wilkinson MD, Dumontier M, Aalbersberg IJ, Appleton G, Axton M, Baak A, et al. The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship. Sci Data. 2016;3:160018. doi:10.1038/sdata.2016.18

**AMA 11** (first three, et al):

> Wilkinson MD, Dumontier M, Aalbersberg IJ, et al. The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship. *Sci Data*. 2016;3:160018. doi:10.1038/sdata.2016.18

The author names, title and DOI are the same in every version, checked against the Crossref record. Only the cut-off point, name order (surname first or initials first) and punctuation change. Style-by-style detail is in our guides to [MLA works cited](/blog/mla-works-cited), [IEEE citation format](/blog/ieee-citation-format) and the [Vancouver style page](/tools/citation-generator/vancouver).

## Is et al. italicized?

**In most styles, no.** APA, MLA, Chicago, Vancouver and AMA print it in roman type, like any common abbreviation [1][2][3]. IEEE italicizes it in reference entries [6], and Cite Them Right Harvard italicizes it in the text and the list [5]. If your style guide shows it in italics in its examples, follow the examples. Whatever you choose, be consistent, and check what your reference manager outputs: some CSL styles italicize it, others do not.

## Et al. with two authors: never

**Two-author works are never shortened with et al. in APA, MLA, Chicago or Harvard.** "Braun et al. (2006)" is wrong for Braun and Clarke (2006), because "and others" implies at least two others. Name both: (Braun & Clarke, 2006) in APA, (Braun and Clarke 77) in MLA.

## Edge cases

### When et al. makes two citations look the same

**If two of your sources would shorten to the same "Name et al., Year", write enough names to tell them apart.** APA's rule is to add names until the citations differ, then et al. [1]. In MLA, you can add a short title to the citation instead [2]. In Chicago author-date, add a, b to the year only if the full author lists are identical. *Example (illustrative):* (Kapoor, Bloom, Montez, et al., 2017) and (Kapoor, Bloom, Zucker, et al., 2017).

### Possessives

"Simmons et al.'s (2011) analysis" is grammatical but awkward. Rewrite: "the analysis by Simmons et al. (2011)" or "Simmons et al. (2011) analyzed...". Some editors forbid the possessive form, so the rewrite is the safe choice.

### Editors and group authors

The same thresholds apply to editors in most styles: in MLA, an edited book with three or more editors is listed under the first editor, then "et al., editors". Group authors are never shortened with et al.: the Open Science Collaboration (2015) is one author, not many.

### Et al. in your own sentences

In running text, "Simmons et al." works only next to a citation. Without one, write "Simmons and colleagues" for readability in theses and reviews.

## Common mistakes with et al.

1. **"et. al."** The period goes after "al." only.
2. **Et al. for two authors.** Always name both.
3. **Et al. in an APA reference list.** APA lists up to 20 authors.
4. **Applying APA thresholds to Harvard or Chicago 17.** Those shorten at four.
5. **Using last edition's Chicago rule.** Chicago 18 (2024) changed in-text shortening from four to three authors, and the bibliography from 10 listed authors to 6. Check which edition your department uses.
6. **Singular verbs and pronouns.** Write "Simmons et al. show... they", not "shows... he".
7. **Mixed italics.** Pick the style's form and keep it.
8. **Wrong first author.** Database exports sometimes reorder authors. Check the article itself.

## FAQ

### What does et al. mean?

Et al. is short for the Latin et alii (or et aliae, et alia), meaning "and others". In citations it replaces the names of coauthors after the first author, so a paper by Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn becomes Simmons et al. Use it for people only. For a list of things, the abbreviation is etc. Each citation style sets how many authors a work needs before you shorten it.

### How do you punctuate et al.?

Write et al. with no period after et and a period after al., because only al. is an abbreviation. Do not write et. al., et.al. or et al without a period, except in AMA style, which drops the period. In APA, put a comma between et al. and the year: (Simmons et al., 2011). At the end of a sentence, the period of al. also ends the sentence.

### When do you use et al. in APA?

In APA 7, use et al. in the text for any work with three or more authors, from the very first citation: (Simmons et al., 2011). Two-author works always name both authors. In the reference list, never use et al.: list up to 20 authors, and for 21 or more, list the first 19, an ellipsis and the last author.

### Do you use et al. in a reference list?

It depends on the style. APA never uses it in the reference list and lists up to 20 authors. MLA uses it in the works cited list for three or more authors. Chicago 18 lists up to six authors and uses the first three plus et al. for seven or more. Vancouver lists six then et al., and AMA lists three then et al when there are more than six.

### Is et al. singular or plural?

Plural. Et al. means "and others", so it always refers to more than one person and takes a plural verb: Simmons et al. show, not Simmons et al. shows. Refer back to the authors as they, not he or she. For a possessive, rewrite the sentence where you can, for example "the analysis by Simmons et al. (2011)" rather than "Simmons et al.'s analysis".

### Is et al. italicized?

Not in APA, MLA, Chicago, Vancouver or AMA, which treat it as a common abbreviation in roman type. Some styles do italicize it: the Cite Them Right version of Harvard italicizes et al. in the text and list, and IEEE italicizes it in reference entries. Follow your style's own examples and keep it the same throughout the paper.
