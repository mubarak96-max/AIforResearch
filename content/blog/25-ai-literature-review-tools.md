# AI literature review tools for analysts, policy writers and UX researchers

*Last updated: 6 November 2026*

An AI literature review tool searches published research, screens and summarizes papers, and helps you pull findings into a table, with a link back to every source. For professional evidence reviews, use one to speed up search, screening and extraction. Keep the question, quality judgments and conclusions for yourself, and check every claim against its paper.

This guide is for people who review evidence for work: market and policy analysts, UX researchers, consultants, health and science writers. It is part of our series on [citing real sources with AI](/blog/ai-citation-generator).

## What an AI literature review tool does

**An AI literature review tool is software that searches published research, screens and summarizes papers, and helps you extract findings, with links back to each source.** The good ones do four jobs:

1. **Search** an index of papers using plain-language questions, not just keywords.
2. **Screen**: rank or filter results against your question and criteria.
3. **Extract**: pull the same fields from each study (sample, method, outcome, effect) into a table.
4. **Synthesize**: draft a summary with citations to the papers it used.

What they do not do is decide whether a finding is strong enough to base a recommendation on. That is the part your reader is paying for.

## Academic vs. professional reviews

A systematic review for a journal and an evidence review for a policy team are different products.

| | Academic systematic review | Professional evidence review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience | Peer reviewers, other researchers | Decision-makers, clients, product teams |
| Time | Months to more than a year | Days to a few weeks |
| Scope | Every eligible study | The best available evidence on a narrow question |
| Sources | Several scholarly databases, exhaustively | Scholarly databases plus grey literature: agency reports, industry data, think-tank papers |
| Reporting standard | PRISMA 2020 (27-item checklist, flow diagram) [1] | A short, transparent methods note |
| Output | Journal article | Brief, memo, report or slide deck |

**A rapid review is an evidence review that simplifies or skips some systematic review steps to deliver a defensible answer in a shorter time.** The Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group has published guidance on which shortcuts are reasonable, such as limiting databases and dates, or using one screener with a second person checking a sample. [2] Most professional reviews are rapid reviews, whether or not they use the name.

## The 6-step rapid review (with where AI helps)

### Step 1: write a narrow question

A good question names the population, the intervention or factor, what you compare it with, and the outcome. "Do four-day work weeks reduce staff turnover in mid-size service companies?" is reviewable. "What does research say about the future of work?" is not.

**AI helps:** ask the AI chat to break your question into sub-questions and list synonyms for each term. You decide which to keep.

### Step 2: search more than one source

Search at least two scholarly indexes and the grey literature your field depends on. For policy that means agency, OECD or World Bank publications; for market work, filings and industry associations; for UX, conference proceedings and practitioner reports.

**AI helps:** semantic search finds papers that use different words for the same idea. **AI misses:** most grey literature is not in the indexes these tools search.

Record the search as you go. Copy this into your notes:

```
Search log
Question:
Date searched:
Source / tool:            Query used:                     Filters:          Results:
Source / tool:            Query used:                     Filters:          Results:
Inclusion criteria:
Exclusion criteria:
```

### Step 3: screen titles and abstracts

Apply your criteria. Tag each result include, exclude or maybe, with a reason.

**AI helps:** tools such as Elicit can screen large result sets against criteria you write. **Check:** screen a random sample of 20 by hand and compare. If you disagree with the tool on more than a few, tighten the criteria.

### Step 4: extract the same fields from every study

Decide the fields before you start. A standard set:

| Field | Example (fictional) |
| --- | --- |
| Citation | Ruiz & Hale, 2024 |
| Setting and sample | 41 UK retail firms, 3,200 staff |
| Design | Before-after, no control group |
| Intervention or factor | 32-hour week, same pay |
| Outcome measured | 12-month voluntary turnover |
| Main result | Turnover fell from 22% to 17% |
| Limits | Self-selected firms; one year |

**AI helps:** extraction into tables is where AI saves the most time. **Check:** verify every number you will quote against the paper, on the page.

### Step 5: synthesize, don't summarize

A synthesis compares studies. Group them by what they found and how strong the design was, then say what the body of evidence supports. "Three before-after studies found lower turnover; the one controlled trial found no difference" is synthesis. A paragraph per paper is not.

### Step 6: write it up for a decision-maker

Lead with the answer and your confidence in it, then the evidence, then the gaps. Add a one-paragraph methods note: sources searched, dates, criteria, and how many studies you included. For structure, see [how to write a research report](/blog/how-to-write-a-research-report).

### Elicit

Elicit is the most complete tool for structured reviews. You write criteria, it screens; you name columns, it extracts. Pro includes a systematic review workflow that screens up to 5,000 papers. ### Consensus

Consensus is fastest when your question has a yes-or-no shape ("Does remote work lower productivity?"). The Consensus Meter summarizes how many studies point each way, and Study Snapshots summarize methods and findings. It exports to RIS and BibTeX. [4] Treat the meter as a starting point: it counts studies, and a count doesn't weigh design quality.

SciSpace combines search, a literature review table and chat with PDF, so you can move from a result to questions about the full text without changing tools. ## Avoiding bias and gaps

AI tools make it easy to stop too early. Five habits keep a fast review honest:

1. **Search at least two indexes.** Each tool covers a different set of papers.
2. **Add grey literature by hand.** Agency reports, industry data and practitioner research rarely show up in scholarly search.
3. **Search for the opposite.** Run your question with "no effect" or "increase" in place of "decrease".
4. **Record every search** with the log above. A reader who doubts your conclusion should be able to rerun it.
5. **Check retractions and preprints** in your final reference list. Preprints may be the newest evidence, but say they are not peer-reviewed.

## Do an AI literature review from PDFs you already have

Many professional reviews start with a folder of reports, not a database search. To review them:

1. Upload them to a tool that answers questions about a PDF and cites pages.
2. Ask the same extraction questions of every document, one at a time: "What was the sample? What was measured? What was the main result, with the number? What limits do the authors state?"
3. Paste answers into your extraction table with the page number.
4. Open the cited page for every number you will quote.

## FAQ

### Can AI write a literature review?

AI can draft one, but it can't be trusted to decide what the evidence says. Use it to search, screen abstracts, extract data into tables and draft summaries with citations to real papers. You still set the question, check that each cited paper supports its sentence, judge study quality and write the conclusions. A review no human has checked is a liability.

### Is an AI literature review reliable?

It is as reliable as its search and your checks. AI tools search one index each, miss grey literature such as agency and industry reports, and sometimes summarize a paper wrongly. Make it reliable by searching more than one source, recording your search, checking a sample of AI extractions against the papers, and reading the key studies yourself.

### What is the best AI literature review tool?

It depends on the job. Elicit is strongest for structured screening and data extraction tables. Consensus is fast for yes-or-no research questions. Scite shows whether later papers support or contrast a finding. SciSpace combines search with PDF chat. WriteGrid is built for the writing stage: drafting the report with cited autocomplete and checking claims.

### Can I do an AI literature review from PDFs I already have?

Yes. Upload the PDFs to a tool that answers questions about a PDF with page numbers, then ask the same extraction questions of every study, one file at a time: sample, method, main finding, limits. Check each answer on the cited page. This works well for grey literature, which scholarly search tools often don't index.

### How long does a rapid evidence review take?

Professional rapid reviews usually take days to a few weeks, compared with many months for a full systematic review. The time saved comes from narrowing the question, limiting databases and dates, and having one reviewer screen with a second checking a sample. AI speeds up screening and extraction, but not the judgment calls.
