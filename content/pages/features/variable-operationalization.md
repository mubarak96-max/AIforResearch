Operationalization of variables is the part of a methodology chapter where a research question becomes measurable. The **Operationalising variables** module in the Thesis Suite does that work as a table: you list your constructs, and each one gets a cited conceptual definition, an operational definition, indicators, an instrument, a level of measurement and the validity and reliability evidence you will need to report. It is for undergraduates, master's and PhD students, and anyone writing a protocol that a supervisor or committee will question line by line.

## What it does

You give it a research question, a list of constructs (one per line, up to twelve) and the approach: quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods. It searches published research for definitions and instruments, then writes four parts.

| Part | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Operationalization table | One row per construct: construct, conceptual definition (cited), operational definition, indicators, instrument, level of measurement, validity and reliability evidence |
| Notes by construct | Alternative measures, adaptation or translation needs, and threats to validity for each construct |
| Variables for analysis | Each variable with its construct, role (independent, dependent, control, moderator or mediator), type, and values or range |
| Checks before data collection | Three to five points: pilot test, permission or licence, translation, reliability in your own sample |

The level of measurement is named for each variable: nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio. Where a summed Likert score is treated as interval data, the table says that this is a convention, not a fact about the scale, because examiners often ask.

For a qualitative study the table has different columns: conceptual definition, how the concept is recognized in the data, indicators or sensitizing questions, the data source, and trustworthiness checks (credibility, dependability, transferability).

### Validated scales from the Survey builder

Where one of your constructs matches a scale the Survey builder can add, the Instrument column names it with "(add it from the Survey builder)" and says whether what the scale measures fits your definition. Six free scales are included, with their published wording and scoring:

| Scale | What it measures |
| --- | --- |
| PHQ-9 | Depression severity |
| GAD-7 | Anxiety severity |
| WHO-5 | Current mental wellbeing |
| PSS-10 | Perceived stress |
| RSES | Global self-esteem |
| SUS | Perceived usability of a product or system |

Other instruments are named only when a cited source describes them. Licensed scales are not included in the Survey builder, and the module does not reproduce their items. A screening score such as a PHQ-9 total is not a diagnosis.

## How it works, step by step

1. Open the Thesis Suite and choose **Operationalising variables** (the route is /thesis?module=operationalisation).
2. Enter your research question, for example "Does perceived stress predict sleep quality in nursing students?"
3. Under **Constructs or variables**, list each construct on its own line.
4. Pick the approach: **Quantitative**, **Qualitative** or **Mixed methods**.
5. Optionally add context: the population, the setting, the language your instruments must be in, or data you already have.
6. Press Run. The module searches published research first, then the table streams in with source markers, and the cited papers appear beside it.
7. Press **Open in editor** to edit the table, keep the citations live and export to Word or PDF for your methodology chapter.

## A worked example

*Example (invented for illustration):* a master's student in nursing asks whether perceived stress predicts sleep quality in nursing students, with social support as a possible moderator.

1. She enters the question and three constructs: perceived stress, sleep quality, social support. Approach: quantitative. Context: "second-year students, one university, questionnaire in English". Cost: 3 credits.
2. The table defines perceived stress from the cited literature and names the PSS-10, "(add it from the Survey builder)", with a note that it measures stress appraisal over the last month, which fits her definition. Its level of measurement is the summed score, treated as interval by convention.
3. For sleep quality and social support the module names instruments described in the sources it found, and where no reliability figure is reported it writes "[report from the instrument's validation study]". She looks those up and checks the licence terms.
4. The Variables for analysis table lists perceived stress as independent, sleep quality as dependent and social support as moderator, with age and year of study as controls.
5. Her supervisor asks for a mixed-methods version for the follow-up interviews. She reruns with **Mixed methods**: 3 credits.
6. She adds the PSS-10 from the Survey builder, collects responses, and runs the regression in [Data Lab](/features/data-lab) for free.

Total: 6 credits, inside the Free plan's 45 a month.

## What it does not do

- It does not choose your constructs or your research question. It works with what you list.
- It does not invent instruments, item wording, cut-offs or reliability coefficients. Gaps are marked for you to fill from the validation studies.
- It does not check licences. Many published instruments need the author's or publisher's permission; the checklist reminds you, but you ask.
- It does not translate or validate a scale for a new population. Translation and adaptation are noted as tasks, not done.
- It does not replace your supervisor's judgment. Read each cited source; a definition can be paraphrased more broadly than the paper states it.
