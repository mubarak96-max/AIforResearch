faq:
  - q: "What are inclusion and exclusion criteria in a systematic review?"
    a: "Inclusion and exclusion criteria are the rules, fixed in the protocol before screening, that decide which studies enter a systematic review. Inclusion criteria state what a study must have, such as the population, intervention, outcome and design. Exclusion criteria name features that rule a study out even if it meets the inclusion criteria, such as an animal population or a conference abstract with no full paper."
  - q: "What is the difference between PICO, PCC and SPIDER?"
    a: "PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) structures questions about the effect of an intervention. PCC (population, concept, context) structures scoping reviews that map what is known. SPIDER (sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, research type) structures qualitative evidence syntheses about experiences and views. Each element becomes a pair of inclusion and exclusion criteria in your protocol."
  - q: "Are exclusion criteria just the opposite of inclusion criteria?"
    a: "No. Restating an inclusion criterion in the negative adds nothing. Exclusion criteria should name specific cases that would otherwise slip through: an eligible population mixed with an ineligible one, a study design that looks similar, a publication type with too little detail, or a language or date limit. If an exclusion line simply negates an inclusion line, delete it."
  - q: "How many inclusion and exclusion criteria should a review have?"
    a: "As many as your framework has elements, plus study design, publication type, and any language or date limits: usually six to ten lines in each list. Fewer than that and screeners will disagree; many more and the criteria start to overlap. Each line should be testable from a title, abstract or methods section without guessing."
  - q: "Should I exclude studies that are not in English?"
    a: "Only if you can justify it, and you must state the limit in your protocol. Language restrictions can bias a review because some studies are published only in other languages. Many teams include all languages at title and abstract and use translation tools at full text. If you do limit languages, record excluded reports under that reason so readers see how many were affected."

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are the rules that decide which studies a systematic review keeps. This builder turns your question into those rules, one element at a time, with tables for PICO (effects), PCC (scoping reviews) and SPIDER (qualitative evidence), each with a worked example. It is for anyone writing a protocol or screening form.

Start from a clear question. If yours is still a topic, work through [how to write a research question](/blog/how-to-write-a-research-question) first. Note that clinical trials also use "inclusion and exclusion criteria" for participants; this page is about criteria for studies in a review.

## Which framework to use

| Your question | Framework | Typical review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does X work, or what is its effect on Y? | PICO: population, intervention, comparison, outcome | Systematic review, meta-analysis, rapid review |
| What is known about X, and how has it been studied? | PCC: population, concept, context | Scoping review, mapping review |
| How do people experience or view X? | SPIDER: sample, phenomenon of interest, design, evaluation, research type | Qualitative evidence synthesis |

The [scoping review vs systematic review](/blog/scoping-review-vs-systematic-review) guide explains the choice in more detail.

## Worked examples (invented)

*All three are invented examples for illustration.*

### PICO: night-shift naps

Question: In adult shift workers, does a planned nap during the night shift improve end-of-shift alertness compared with no nap?

| Element | Include | Exclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Population | Adults (18+) working night or rotating shifts | Simulated shifts with no work duties |
| Intervention | A planned nap of any length during the shift | Naps taken only before the shift |
| Comparison | No nap, or breaks without sleep | No comparison group |
| Outcomes | Alertness at end of shift (PVT, KSS) | |
| Study design | Randomized and non-randomized controlled studies, including crossover | Case series, cross-sectional surveys |
| Publication type | Full reports in journals or theses | Conference abstracts with no full paper |

### PCC: digital tools for medication adherence

Question: What digital tools have been studied to support medication adherence in older adults living in the community? (The topic of the scoping example in [scoping review vs systematic review](/blog/scoping-review-vs-systematic-review).)

| Element | Include | Exclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Population | Adults aged 65 or over | Studies where results for over-65s cannot be separated |
| Concept | Any digital tool aimed at medication adherence: apps, text reminders, smart pill boxes | Tools for prescribing staff only |
| Context | Community and home settings | Hospital inpatients, care homes |
| Source types | Primary studies of any design, reviews, government reports | Opinion pieces |

### SPIDER: nurses' experience of night-shift naps

Question: How do nurses experience taking planned naps during night shifts?

| Element | Include | Exclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sample | Registered nurses working night shifts | Mixed staff groups where nurses' views cannot be separated |
| Phenomenon of interest | Taking, or being unable to take, planned naps at work | Sleep outside work hours |
| Design | Interviews, focus groups, open-ended survey questions analyzed qualitatively | Closed-question surveys |
| Evaluation | Experiences, views, barriers and facilitators | |
| Research type | Qualitative and mixed methods with a separate qualitative analysis | Quantitative only |

## Rules that make criteria work

1. **One idea per line.** "Adults with insomnia treated in primary care" is three criteria. Split them so a screener can tell which one failed.
2. **Testable from the text.** A screener must be able to answer Yes, No or Unclear from the abstract or methods. "Clinically relevant outcomes" is not testable; "alertness measured with the PVT or KSS" is.
3. **No quality criteria.** Don't screen for "high-quality studies". Assess risk of bias after inclusion, so readers see the whole evidence base. See [levels of evidence](/blog/levels-of-evidence-and-study-types) for how designs are ranked.
4. **Exclusions add information.** Use them for edge cases, not to repeat the inclusion line in the negative.
5. **Justify limits.** State and justify every language, date or country limit in the protocol.
6. **Pilot, then fix.** Two reviewers apply the criteria to 50 to 100 records. Rewrite any criterion they disagree on before formal screening, and date the change.

## How to use the criteria in AI for Research

In the Systematic Review workspace, the criteria you enter drive the protocol draft, the AI screening suggestions and the methods draft.

1. Choose **New review**, enter the question and pick the **Type of review**. The framework follows the type: PICO for systematic, rapid, meta-analysis and living reviews; PCC for scoping, mapping and realist reviews; SPIDER for qualitative evidence synthesis. You can switch it.
2. Fill the framework fields. PICO gives Population, Intervention or exposure, Comparison and Outcomes; PCC gives Population, Concept and Context; SPIDER gives Sample, Phenomenon of interest, Design, Evaluation and Research type.
3. Type your criteria under **Include when** and **Exclude when**, one per line, up to 40 of each.
4. On the **Search** tab, the PubMed search box starts from your framework fields, each in parentheses, joined by AND. Rewrite it into a proper search string with synonyms and subject headings.
5. When you screen, **Suggest for the next 25** (1 credit) reads each record against these criteria and suggests include, exclude with a reason, or maybe.

## What this builder and the workspace do not do

- **They do not choose your criteria.** The builder gives structure and the AI drafts from what you type; the eligibility decisions are yours and your team's.
- **The starting search string is a rough start.** It joins your framework words with AND; it does not add synonyms, MeSH terms or field tags. See [systematic review search strategy](/blog/systematic-review-search-strategy).
- **AI suggestions read titles and abstracts only.** They can't apply a criterion that needs the methods section, so expect more Maybe suggestions on design and outcomes.
- **Other frameworks are not built in.** PICOS, PICOT, PICo, ECLIPSE and SPICE aren't framework options in the workspace; put their extra elements in the Include when and Exclude when lines.
