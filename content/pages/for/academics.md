AI for professors is most useful on the work that piles up: reading student drafts, writing grant proposals, and checking a manuscript before it goes out. AI for Research gives academics a Reviewer that scores a draft at the right level, a Proposal Studio for grant formats, claim and citation checks for manuscripts, and projects shared with students and co-authors.

## Three jobs you can do here

### 1. Give students structured feedback on drafts

Where your institution allows it, upload a student's chapter to the **Reviewer**, choose the document type (thesis, proposal or manuscript) and the level (undergraduate, honours, master's or PhD). You get a score out of 10 for each criterion and major and minor comments, each quoting the passage with a suggested fix. Use it as a first read that frees your own reading for the argument. Standard costs 10 credits per 10,000 words.

### 2. Draft grant proposals in your funder's format

**Proposal Studio** writes a grant proposal in a generic, NIH, NSF, ERC or Wellcome shape. Paste the call, its criteria and limits, and your idea. A whole draft costs 15 credits; one section (aims, significance, approach, budget justification) costs 3. It also drafts ethics applications (IRB, UK REC, HREC) and data management plans.

### 3. Check a manuscript before submission

Run **Claim check** on key paragraphs (2 credits): each claim is labeled supported, partly supported, not supported or no evidence, with the papers. Run the **citation check** on the reference list (free): it confirms each reference exists and flags retractions, corrections, expressions of concern and wrong years or authors. Then format to the journal: 10,000+ citation styles, Word export, and LaTeX export on paid plans.

## How it works, step by step

1. **Create a project** per student, grant or paper. Invite students as Can edit on their own project, co-supervisors as Can comment.
2. **Review a draft.** In the Reviewer, upload the file (PDF or Word), pick My documents, or paste text. Choose type, level and checks, and describe the formatting rules if you want them checked.
3. **Read the result** beside the draft: the scorecard, then major comments, then minor. Copy the comments you agree with into your own feedback.
4. **Start a grant.** In Proposal Studio choose Grant proposal and the funder, paste the call, and run one section or the whole proposal. Open it in the editor.
5. **Ground the background.** Run a Deep report (15 credits) on the gap the grant addresses, save the cited papers, and cite them in the funder's style.
6. **Check before submitting.** Claim check on the significance section, citation check on the reference list, export to Word or LaTeX.

## AI-use rules that matter to you

- **Peer review.** NIH prohibits reviewers from using generative AI to analyze or critique applications, and uploading application content breaches confidentiality [1]. NSF extends reviewers' confidentiality duty to AI tools [2]. Never upload material you are reviewing.
- **Grant applications.** NIH does not treat applications substantially developed by AI as the applicant's original ideas [3]. NSF says proposers are responsible for AI-assisted content and encourages them to say how AI was used [2].
- **Papers.** ICMJE asks journals to require disclosure of AI use, and says chatbots cannot be authors and humans are responsible for all content [4].
- **Student work.** Follow your institution's rules on processing student work with AI, and tell students when you use it.

Rules change: check the funder's or journal's current page before each submission. For a lab policy built on these rules, see [AI tools for research labs](/blog/ai-tools-for-research-labs).

## What it does not do

- It does not grade. Scores are feedback, not marks, and the Reviewer can miss problems an expert sees.
- It does not submit to funders or journals, or fill their online forms.
- It does not search Google Scholar, Scopus or Web of Science.
- Team is not self-serve: it is set up through sales.
