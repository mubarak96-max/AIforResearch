A patent search tool for researchers should put patents next to the papers, because the same idea often appears in both. AI for Research looks up granted US patents through USPTO PatentsView in Official Sources, free of charge, and searches 250M+ scholarly works for the literature on the same technology. You see who patented what and when, and what the science says, before you talk to your technology transfer office or a patent attorney.

It is a research tool. Nothing here is a patentability, validity or freedom-to-operate opinion; a registered patent attorney or patent agent decides those.

## Tools for law and patents

A useful habit is to run the same idea twice: once as a patent lookup and once as a literature question. Inventors often publish before or after they file, and a paper can explain a method that the patent claims only in legal language. Keep both in the same project so the evidence stays together.

## A worked example

*Example (invented for illustration):* a postdoc in materials engineering has a new coating for medical implants and a meeting with her university's technology transfer office next week.

1. In Official Sources she searches PatentsView for antibacterial implant coatings granted in the last ten years and notes 14 US patents, with their assignees. 0 credits.
2. A Deep Research Summary answer on "which antibacterial coating approaches for titanium implants have been tested in animal studies?" cites nine papers. 1 credit.
3. A Deep report on silver-based versus peptide-based coatings, for the background section of her disclosure form: 15 credits.
4. She records five of the closest patents in the Technical research workbook, each with the claim number and the passage she read. 0 credits.
5. She brings the patent list, the papers and her notes to the technology transfer office. Their patent attorney runs the formal search, including European and international filings, and advises on patentability.

Total: 16 credits, inside the Free plan's 45 a month.

## Citation styles and reporting standards

Legal writing in the US mostly uses the Bluebook, and in the UK OSCOLA; the editor formats references in 10,000+ citation styles. <!-- VERIFY: Bluebook and OSCOLA CSL styles are available and render legal cases correctly --> Cite a US patent by its number, title, inventor and issue date, and note the date you checked it. In a technical paper, patents are cited like any other source in the journal's style.

## What it does not do

- It gives no patentability, validity, infringement or freedom-to-operate opinion, and no legal advice of any kind. A patent attorney or agent decides.
- It searches US patents only: no EPO, WIPO, JPO or CNIPA, no patent families and no legal status.
- It is not a complete prior art search. Prior art also includes non-US filings, products, theses and other public disclosures.
- It does not draft patent claims or file applications.
- It does not search case law, statutes or legal databases such as Westlaw or Lexis. Legal scholarship comes through the normal paper sources and the Web source.
